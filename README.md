# Hotdog Simulator

A first-person kitchen survival game with five detailed hotdog players. Survive one zombie alley-cat wave together. When the wave is cleared, the food emergency begins: **the only food left is the other hotdogs**. Friendly fire turns on, everyone's HP drains from hunger, and eating a rival restores health. The last hotdog standing wins.

That is the siege. The second mode is **[Forts](#forts)**: two teams of three, the Mustard Eaters and the Ketchup Connoisseurs, build a fortress each behind a forcefield, then fight to steal the bacon from the middle and carry it home. The third is **[Free For All](#free-for-all-and-the-sandbox)**, everyone against everyone. The three gun modes play by the same [basics](#basics-every-gun-mode) (the loadout, the pickups, F, the score for a kill); each adds only its objective. **The mode is the game's choice, not the map's** (the lobby's, `?mode=` in the address, the server's `--mode`): a map's own `mode` is only the mode it plays when nothing else says. Free For All and the siege play on every gun map, Forts on any map with the Forts layout (two bases, a field and a bacon spot: the four Forts maps). Two more modes are outside this: **[Fully Loaded](#fully-loaded)**, a card game the hotdogs play round a little table on top of Counter Siege's microwave, and **[Kung Fu](#kung-fu)**, on the dojo. The sixth, **[Titan](#titan)**, is a boss fight on the kitchen floor at night: one giant hotdog, twelve hotdogs tall, against all the others, who play by the gun basics; it has [a map of its own](#the-arena-titans-kitchen), where the siege and Free For All may be played too.

## Five-player demo

```sh
cargo build --release --target x86_64-unknown-linux-gnu --target-dir target-native
./target-native/x86_64-unknown-linux-gnu/release/hotdog-simulator --demo
```

All five hotdogs are active AI players in the demo. The camera shows their detailed bodies and identity colors. Each starts with the syrup rifle in hand and the mustard pistol beside it. During the warm-up they fetch the guns lying about (two ketchup snipers, the hot sauce thrower, the relish bazooka, a can of bug spray), each trading its pistol for one, then they clear exactly six zombies with them and their rifles, an alert announces the food shortage and free for all, and an opening betrayal demonstrates friendly fire (FRANKIE fires on MUSTARD with its pistol if it still has one, and otherwise a burst from its rifle). Each hotdog's name and HP float over its head (see [the head-up display](#the-head-up-display)). Hunger steadily lowers HP; hotdogs hunt, shoot, bite, and consume fallen rivals to recover health. The demo declares one winner and replays after eight seconds (now and then the last two hotdogs kill each other in the same instant, and the round has no winner). Enter also restarts a finished round.

For interactive play, use `--skip-intro`: you control one hotdog alongside four bots. Two yellow-edged ramps lead from the counter to the raised turret deck. Walk up either ramp; jumping is not required.

## Controls

These are the default keys; every one of them can be changed in the [menu](#menu--controls). Click the game to capture the mouse. WASD moves (a person's hotdog walks at 1.25 m/s, a bot's at 0.75 m/s; crouched 65% of that, prone 40%, stimmed 150%, carrying something heavy 80% or less; Kung Fu keeps its own, slower pace: see [Kung Fu](#kung-fu)); Space jumps; Esc opens the [menu](#menu--controls). E collects a gun the hotdog does not hold (walking over the pickup of a gun it holds, or over a box of ammunition, takes the rounds without a key: see [pickups](#pickups)), eats a nearby hotdog that is already dead, or, at the dial of an [electric griddle](#the-electric-griddle), switches it on or off. Left mouse fires. R reloads the gun in hand from its reserve (see [magazines](#weapons)). F is the melee strike, made with whatever is in hand (a pistol whip, a rifle butt...): from behind a rival it kills, from anywhere else it takes half their HP (see [melee](#weapons)); during free for all it is the bite. Hold the right mouse button to aim (or click it once up and once down, as the menu's Scope setting has it): with the ketchup sniper it is the scope, and with the syrup rifle the rifle's optic comes up to the eye (see [the syrup rifle](#weapons)). Hold Ctrl (or C) to crouch. T drops prone or stands back up. Shift slaps on a stim.

A hotdog carries two guns, in two slots, and always has one of them in hand: there are no bare hands. **1** takes the primary in hand, **2** the secondary, and **Y** swaps between them (with one gun, Y does nothing). The **mouse wheel** changes guns as Y does: rolled down it takes the next gun, rolled up the one before (with two guns either way swaps). One roll is one change, whatever a browser makes of a notch (Chrome sends 100 px, Firefox 3 lines): a trackpad's small scrolls add up to a change at 50 px, a change takes everything scrolled so far with it, the wheel is not heard for 0.15 s after one (a fling changes the gun every 0.15 s at the most), and a part of a notch is let go when the scrolling turns round or stops for 0.3 s. In a browser, rolling the wheel with Ctrl held (as a crouching player does) does not zoom the page. The wheel changes no gun while the hands are full (carrying a loose object it turns the object instead: see below), while dead (it zooms the death camera then), at the Fully Loaded table, or while the page's chat box has the keyboard. The primary is the bigger gun (the syrup rifle, the ketchup sniper, the relish bazooka or the hot sauce thrower, in that order), the secondary the mustard pistol or the bug spray; holding two big guns, the second of them is the secondary, and a gun held alone goes in the slot of its kind (a mustard pistol alone is on 2, and 1 is empty). **Every hotdog starts with the syrup rifle in hand as its primary, on 1, and the mustard pistol as its secondary, on 2**, in every gun mode (the siege, Forts and Free For All), person and bot alike, in the demo, in single player and on the server. The rifle has a full magazine of 60 rounds and 180 in reserve, the pistol a magazine of 12 and 48 in reserve (see [basics](#basics-every-gun-mode)); the sandbox's own `weapons=` still apply on top. To collect a gun and keep the rifle, take the pistol in hand first (2). Collecting a third gun replaces the one in hand, and that gun's ammunition is lost. There is no knife. The head-up display shows the gun in hand as its silhouette with a bullet, the rounds in its magazine and those in reserve, and the other gun small over it with its rounds (see [the head-up display](#the-head-up-display)).

In a browser Ctrl+W closes the tab and the page cannot prevent it, so crouch-walking forwards is safer on C there.

Q picks up the loose object the hotdog is looking at or standing beside, and puts down the one in its hands; with something in its hands the trigger throws it, the **mouse wheel turns it** an eighth of a turn a notch (rolled down, clockwise seen from above) and **X tips it** over onto its front, then onto its side, then back, so that a cutting board or a cereal box stands up as a wall panel (see [carrying](#carrying)). R reloads; in a Forts build phase, while the guns are locked and there is nothing to reload, R says "ready" instead. H shows or hides the [hints](#hints), which are off to begin with: the game is played from the head-up display's icons, and assumes these keys are known.

Multiplayer in a browser adds two keys: Enter opens the text chat (Enter sends, Esc closes) and V is push to talk. In the lobby the mouse looks around without being captured, and Ready is a button on the page (Enter in the native client). See [the lobby](#the-lobby).

### Menu & controls

**Esc opens the menu** (`src/menu.rs`), over the game: in a round, dead, at the card table and in the fridge between online rounds. In a browser Esc is the browser's own way out of the pointer lock and the page never hears it, so there the rule is: the menu opens when the pointer lock ends while the page still has the keyboard (checked 0.12 s later). Losing the lock to another window or tab, to the page's chat box, or because the game let go of it (a round over) does not open it, and Esc with the pointer already free opens it too. At the card table the first Esc only frees the pointer to click the scoreboard, as it always has, and the next opens the menu. While it is open the game hears no key, button, mouse motion, wheel or touch; **single player is paused**, and **online the round goes on** with the hotdog standing still. **Resume** is a click, which captures the pointer again (a browser gives the lock only for a click) and is not heard as a shot; natively Esc resumes too. Esc inside the menu cancels a rebinding, then goes back a page, then closes it.

- **Settings**: mouse sensitivity (0.2 to 3 times the game's), invert Y (the mouse in first person, round the death camera and at the card table; a touch drag keeps the page's speed), the field of view (65 to 105 degrees top to bottom, 85 to begin with; the scope stays 18), the volume (every sound; the game has no music), the hints, the damage indicators (the arcs round the crosshair, see [the head-up display](#the-head-up-display)), kill slow motion, the scope (hold, to begin with, or toggle: whether the right button holds the sniper's scope and the rifle's optic up while it is down, or one click puts them up and the next takes them down), and the graphics quality (as the page's menu and F8 have it). In a browser also the resolution, and the renderer, which is a build of its own: a change says it takes effect when the page reloads and offers RELOAD NOW. Each applies at once and is remembered (`look.sensitivity`, `look.invert`, `fov`, `volume`, `damage.indicators`, `kill.slowmo`, `scope.toggle`, `hints`, natively `graphics`, in `src/prefs.rs`'s store). **Kill slow motion** (on to begin with, `src/slowmo.rs`): when this player's shot or blow kills a hotdog (in Kung Fu, when its blow lands the KO), time slows to a quarter for about 0.6 s of real time and eases back, while the camera pushes in a little towards the victim, the picture loses colour and gains contrast, the edges darken and the sounds are pitched down; kills in a row hold it longer, never past 1.2 s, and another may not start for a second after one ends. Single player slows for real: the round, the bots, the rounds in the air and the player's own walk. Online the round's clock is the server's and is never touched: only the victim's fall, the camera, the colour and the sounds slow, on this screen alone, while the controls, the other hotdogs and the rounds go on at their pace. A death that ends a round is shown the same way, to everybody.
- **Controls** (`src/controls.rs`): every action in seven tabs, Movement, Combat, Weapons, Carrying, [Kung Fu](#kung-fu)'s two, Kung Fu and Styles, and [Titan](#titan)'s, with a key and an alternate each (and a replay's two, Replay and Replay camera). Click one and press a key, a mouse button or roll the wheel (Esc cancels). An input already used by an action heard at the same time is refused with the action that has it, and SWAP trades the two (the trigger and the throw can share the left button, the guns and the turning of what is carried the wheel, the reload and Forts' ready R, which is only heard in the build phase while the guns are locked, and Kung Fu's strikes, kick, block, grab, dodge, special and styles the keys of the guns, the aim, the melee, picking up, the stim and prone, none of which is heard in Kung Fu, as the titan's swat, ground smash, stomp and charge share the trigger's, the aim's, the melee's and the stim's, none of which the giant hears; the view over the shoulder, V, is Kung Fu's and the titan's). Right-click clears one; RESET puts one action back, RESET ALL everything. INVERT MOUSE WHEEL turns the wheel round for the guns and for what is carried. Left and right Shift, Ctrl and Alt count as one key; Esc can never be bound, and a left click still captures a free pointer whatever the trigger is. The bindings are remembered (`controls`: only what differs from the defaults; `controls.wheel`). The head-up display's key caps and the touch controls' buttons follow them; the hints' sentences still name the default keys.
- **Licence and source**: under the menu, in small print, the game says it is GPL-3.0-only and where its source is (`Cargo.toml`'s `repository`, or `HOTDOG_SOURCE_URL` when the game is compiled with it); in a browser THIRD-PARTY LICENCES opens `THIRD_PARTY_LICENSES.html` in a new tab, natively it states the file's path (beside the `assets/` folder).
- **Leaving**: natively QUIT GAME; in a browser LEAVE MATCH online (the page reloads into the lobby, which seats you for the next round) and RESTART offline (the page's links choose another mode: they are hidden while the game is on the screen, where they would cover its display, and shown again with this menu, as is THIRD-PARTY LICENCES in it). Each asks once more.

### Gamepad

A gamepad the system knows (an Xbox controller by cable or Bluetooth) plays the native game (`src/gamepad.rs`; in a browser it is compiled in but has not been tried): left stick walks, right stick looks, the triggers fire and aim, the bumpers use and carry, A jumps, B crouches, X is ready, Y swaps, the sticks pressed are stim and melee, the D-pad is hints, prone, primary and secondary, and Start opens the menu (which the mouse works). SETTINGS has CONTROLLER: AUTO plays with whatever is touched, KEYBOARD + MOUSE never listens to a pad, GAMEPAD always does. A phone's touch controls and a headset's controllers come with the device.

### Touch

On a phone or a tablet (a touch screen and no mouse) the page lays touch controls over the game; on a computer with a touch screen and a mouse they come up at the first touch, and `?touch` or `?touch=0` in the address turns them on or off whatever the device. Hold the phone sideways; in portrait the same controls stand higher, above the weapon line, and a note suggests turning it.

- **Left thumb: walk.** Wherever it lands on the left of the screen a stick comes up under it, and how far it is pushed is how fast the hotdog walks: past a small dead zone, a full push is the walking pace and a push of a little over half the stick's reach is half of it.
- **Anywhere else: look.** A drag turns the view (nothing is captured), at the look speed set in the menu: 3 by default, at which a 100-pixel drag turns 38°, a third of that through the sniper's scope, and 1/2.2 of it through the syrup rifle's optic (as many times slower as it magnifies).
- **Fire** (bottom right) holds the trigger for as long as it is held, and a thumb that slides off it keeps firing and turns the view as it goes, so walking, aiming and firing take three thumbs at once (or two). Carrying something it says Throw; behind the forcefield, Locked.
- Round it: **Jump** (Stand when prone), **Melee** (Bite in the siege's famine), **Stim**, and **Crouch** and **Prone**, which are toggles. **Scope** is there while the ketchup sniper or the syrup rifle is in hand (the rifle's puts its optic to the eye); a tap puts it up and the next takes it down, whatever the menu's Scope setting (a thumb cannot hold it and aim too). The guns are a column on the left, under the menu button: **1** and **2** for the slots that hold a gun, and **Swap** when both do.
- Buttons come and go with what can be done: **Pick up** (E) at a weapon or ammunition pickup, **Eat** (E) beside a dead hotdog in the siege's famine, **Carry** (Q) within reach of a loose object and **Put down** while carrying it, **Ready** (R) in a Forts build phase, **Hints** in a round (top right: it hides the hints and shows them again, as H does; it reads Hide hints while they show), **New round** (Enter) when a single-player round is over, and, dead, **Next** in Fire's place while there is a living hotdog to watch. Dead, a drag also takes the death camera round and two fingers pinched apart or together bring it nearer or take it further off.
- **☰** opens the menu: the look speed, Chat (online), and hiding the controls (a Touch controls button brings them back, and the browser remembers); while it is open the links to the other modes, which the controls otherwise cover, are under it, and the frame-rate line at the top takes taps for its [graphics menus](#the-graphics-menus).
- **In the lobby** a drag looks round the fridge, the panel scrolls, and Chat (top right) opens the chat box at the top of the screen, clear of the on-screen keyboard, whose Enter sends.
- **As the titan** ([Titan](#titan)) the buttons are its moves: **Swat** (held), **Smash**, **Stomp**, **Charge** (greyed while it cools down), **Jump** and **Crouch**; none of the guns'.
- **At the card table** ([Fully Loaded](#fully-loaded)) there are two buttons and no stick: **Flip** under the right thumb and **Stop** under the left, far apart, greyed while it is not this player's go. While this player picks who gets an action card it drew, Flip says **Pick** and Stop says **Me**: a drag anywhere else turns the view round the table, Pick gives the card to the hotdog looked at (its tag lights up white), and Me gives it to this player's own hotdog (the one way to pick oneself by touch, as there is no pointer to click the scoreboard and one's own seat is not to be looked at). The menu and Chat are top right, clear of the scoreboard.
- While the controls are on the page does not scroll, zoom or select text, and a long press brings up no menu. A mouse that captures the pointer puts them away until it lets go.

The page keeps the stick, the drag and the buttons in `window.hotdogTouch` (`touch.js`, `touch.css`). Once a frame `src/touch.rs` reads it and presses the keys the buttons stand for in Bevy's own keyboard and mouse input (the scope is the right mouse button), so everything that reads the keys plays by touch with nothing of its own; hands the stick, the drag and the trigger to the round's reading of the keys and the mouse (`arena::tick`), and the lobby a cursor; and tells the page what this player can do now, a few bits sent when they change, which decide the buttons shown. The head-up display's prompts by the crosshair leave their key caps out while the controls are on: the buttons say it.

### The head-up display

The head-up display is minimal, for players who know the buttons: icons and numbers, and words only for names and for a moment of one to three words in the middle of the screen (`src/hud.rs`, its icons `src/icons.rs`). It grows with the window: its sizes are given for a 720-pixel window, where an icon is 20 to 32 pixels high, and each icon is drawn for the pixels it takes, so it stays crisp at any size.

- **Bottom left**: a heart, the health bar (green when full, amber at half, red when nearly gone) and the number. Over it the abilities, each an icon: the stim (bright when ready, dim with its seconds while it comes back, lit with its seconds left while it lasts), and while they apply the stance (crouching, prone), Free For All's spawn shield, an infection and the famine's hunger.
- **Bottom right**: the gun in hand as its own silhouette (the side of its model), a bullet, the rounds in its magazine and, small after them, its reserve (the magazine amber when a quarter full or less, red when empty, green for a moment after a pickup; the reserve red when there is none; infinity where rounds never run out); while it reloads, a thin amber bar under them that fills; for a moment after walking over a pickup that put rounds into a reserve, the box's icon and +N in green over them; and over them the other gun, small, with its rounds (magazine and reserve together). Behind the forcefield a lock is on the gun. With the hands full, a hand, or the bacon. (In a browser it stands a little higher, clear of the page's frame-rate line.)
- **Top middle**: the score and the clock. The siege: the wave's clock (then the cats left), the hotdogs alive and this player's kills. Free For All: the clock, this player's kills of the 25 to win, and the leader's kills under a crown. Forts: each team's rounds as three pips in its colour either side of the build clock (a hammer) and the players ready (a tick, with an R key cap until this player is), and in the fight, under them, the objective: the bacon (carrying it, home) with an arrow pointing the way and the distance, the bacon's seconds back to its spot when dropped, and the stalemate's seconds before the cats come.
- **Top right**: the kill feed, small, for five seconds a kill: the killer as an icon in its colour (a hotdog; or a cat, a turret, flies), what killed (the gun's silhouette, a strike, a backstab, a bite, a flame for a hot griddle), and the victim's name.
- **By the crosshair**, only while it applies: a key cap and an icon for what the key does here: [E] and the gun on a pad (a gun this player does not hold: the pickup of one it holds, and a box of ammunition, are taken by walking over them), [Q] and a hand at something loose (a napkin coming off at a draped wall), and carrying [Q] and where it goes (down, locked, draped over so many) and the left button to throw; in the famine [E] to eat a fallen hotdog and [F] to bite one in reach; [E] and a power symbol (amber while it is on) within reach of an [electric griddle](#the-electric-griddle)'s dial, facing it. On an empty pad, its pickup's icon and the seconds until it is back. By touch there are no key caps: the buttons say it.
- **Round the crosshair**, when this player is hit (`src/damage.rs`): an arc on a ring that points where the hit came from, ahead at the top, behind at the bottom, turning as the player turns and walks, whole for a quarter of a second and gone 1.2 s after the last hit from there; each source its own arc, brighter for more damage. A round, a burst, a swarm or the turret is red, a blow or a bite a wider rosy arc, a Kung Fu blow wide and orange-red, a hot griddle orange at the bottom of the ring (from below); hunger, a fall, and a hit from straight above or below pulse the whole ring faintly instead. The edge of the screen nearest the hit flashes a soft red. The settings turn these damage indicators off. The [giant](#titan) is under fire all match and is shown its hits otherwise: as the share of its health they take, one soft, faint cue for each way they come from (whoever fired), out on a wide ring clear of the crosshair and of the giant in its third person, with no flash of the edges; a hit on the back of its head is a thick gold cue pointing at the shooter, with the weak spot's icon, and the edge on that side flashes gold; a stagger pulses every edge once. When this player's shot or blow hurts a hotdog, four small white ticks flash round the crosshair (the hit marker); a kill, of a hotdog or a cat, makes them bigger and red and turns the crosshair into a red cross for a moment. A kill can also bring a moment of [slow motion](#menu--controls).
- **Titan** ([below](#titan)): for a small hotdog, the giant's health bar across the top under its name (TITAN, or its player's name), with the stagger meter its legs fill under the bar, a gold edge and two chevrons down while it is on its knees, and the weak spot's icon by its name while the back of its head is open; under it the clock, the reinforcements left as pips, this player's damage to the giant and its weak-spot hits. By the crosshair, while the giant winds up a move and strikes with it, the move's icon (red when it is coming at this player: a charge down its line, a smash or a landing within reach, a sweep at its height; amber otherwise) over a bar running out to the blow, and an arrow on a ring round the crosshair pointing at the giant; and while the back of its head is open to this player, a gold target up and right of the crosshair, pulsing, with an arrow to it (faster, and no arrow, with the crosshair on it). The player who is the giant has its own health bottom left (its thousands, the stagger under it), its five moves over it as abilities (each bright when ready, dim with its seconds while it cools down, lit while it is made), and at the top the clock, the small hotdogs alive and the pool's pips; no gun, no rounds, no stim. The giant wears no floating name tag (the boss bar names it); to its player the small hotdogs' tags show across the whole room, over the rings round their feet. The feed shows the giant's kills with the move that made each, and every weak-spot hit: the shooter, the weak spot, and the damage.
- **The middle**: a moment, a few words for a few seconds: WAVE, FREE FOR ALL, BUILD, STEAL THE BACON, RUN HOME, INFECTED, YOU DIED (in Free For All with the seconds to the respawn), and at a round's end YOU WIN, NAME WINS, NO SURVIVORS or DRAW (offline with an ENTER key cap for a new round), in Forts ROUND WON, ROUND LOST, VICTORY or DEFEAT; in Titan ON ITS KNEES (STAGGERED for the giant), OUT once the pool is empty, and TITAN DOWN, THE TITAN WINS, YOU WIN or FELLED.

Health is also shown over the hotdogs (`src/tags.rs`). Over every other hotdog floats a tag: its name over a bar of its HP, green when full, amber at half and red when nearly gone, the name and the tag's edge in the hotdog's colour (in a siege its own, the colour of its legs and badge; in Forts its team's). The tag sits just over the head (in Forts over the flag on its pole) and keeps to it; it is a little larger near and smaller far (its name 10 to 15 pixels high, in whole pixels), fades out while the hotdog is behind a block, a raised deck or a loose object (not behind the forcefield, which is glass) and between 3.6 and 4.6 m away, and is not drawn off the screen or up among the score at the top, the alert or the hints; a nearer tag is drawn over a further one. A hotdog that dies shows OUT for a couple of seconds and then nothing; an eaten one shows nothing. In Forts the bacon's carrier says BACON. The spectator and the demo show everybody's tags.

**Dead**, the player has no first person. Within a quarter of a second of dying the view is out of its eyes, above and behind its body, looking down at it, and then circles it slowly (`src/deathcam.rs`). The camera is the player's to move: with the pointer captured (dead, the first click only captures it) the mouse takes it round whatever it watches, all the way round and from 9° to 72° above it, and the wheel brings it as near as 0.2 m or takes it as far as 1 m off (it settles 0.45 m off and 41° above); a click or Space shows the next living hotdog, and the next after that, round again (in a siege or Free For All anybody, in Forts a team mate). It starts on the player's own body. Walls and blocks bring it nearer and higher, as they always have, and stop the mouse. Left alone for three seconds it eases back into circling the body slowly, or, on another hotdog, into following it from behind. For the first half second nothing the mouse or the keys do moves it, so the fight it died in does not. Its hands and gun are not drawn, and of the head-up display only the score, the clock, the objective and the kill feed are left, with an eye, the name of the hotdog watched and the left button (the next) along the bottom. The health, the abilities, the gun, the prompts, the hints and the tooltip, the crosshair and hit marker and the scope go, and the player's own body has no tag (everybody else's show as usual). All of it comes back with the next life.

### Hints

The hints are help for a new player and are **off to begin with**; H (on a touch screen the small Hints button) turns them on and off, and the choice is remembered for the next game: in a browser in its storage (`hotdog.hints`), natively in a small settings file (`$XDG_CONFIG_HOME/hotdog-simulator/settings.json`, or `~/.config/...`, or `%APPDATA%\hotdog-simulator\settings.json` on Windows; `HOTDOG_SETTINGS` names another; `src/prefs.rs`). The menu's Settings page turns them on and off too (`Hints::set`), and the choice is remembered the same way. With the hints on, the words come back: the panel, the tooltip, the alert's banner and the lines saying what just happened (a pickup, an infection, how a round starts).

A small panel under the score and the objective at the top of the screen (under the alert, when that is up) says what the round is about and gives one tip at a time, the next every 6 seconds and the first again as soon as the situation changes (`src/hints.rs`). ### Weapons

Guns squirt condiments: visible rounds that fly until they meet something solid, the counter, a body, or the end of their range. They are fast (five times as fast as they once were) but not instant: a rival far enough off can still step out of the way, and bots lead their targets (as people do, imperfectly: see [bots](#bots)). The simulation owns every hit. A pillow does not stop a round, and the ketchup sniper's round goes on through bodies: see [materials and rounds](#materials-and-rounds).

The speeds are five times what they were (`feast::SPEEDUP`); every reach is as it was, so a round is in the air a fifth as long. **Magazines and reloading** (`crates/kitchen-sim/src/magazine.rs`). The trigger takes rounds from the gun's magazine; besides it each gun has a reserve, up to the most it carries. **R** reloads the gun in hand: for its reload time it cannot fire, and then as many rounds as the magazine has room for go into it from the reserve. Taking another gun in hand (or a loose object, a melee strike or dying) cancels a reload, and nothing goes in. Pulling the trigger on an empty magazine reloads it, if the reserve has anything; with nothing left at all the gun stays in hand, empty, and still strikes (F). In first person the gun is tipped over to the free hand, the magazine is pulled and dropped away (the pistol's squeeze bottle comes up off its slide, the sniper's and the thrower's bottles and the rifle's jug drop out below, the bazooka gets a fresh glob pushed into its mouth), a fresh one is seated with a jolt, and the slide, bolt, charging handle or pump is worked; the bug spray and the mayonnaise bottle are swapped whole and shaken or squeezed. Seen from outside, the free hand goes to the magazine, out, down and back. It clicks out, clacks home and racks (`tools/build_reload_sounds.py`). Every gun mode starts the guns with a full magazine and spare rounds in reserve (see [basics](#basics-every-gun-mode)). Bots reload too: at once when a magazine runs dry, and a half-empty one when their target is out of reach. Online a frame carries each gun's magazine as it always has; the reserves and the reload under way go to every client as `{type:'ammo',hotdogs:[...]}` when they change, and R travels in a command as both of the pose's tip bits at once (`button::RELOAD`, a tip no pose has), so protocol 12's frames and `net::STAGES` are as they were.

Rounds fired during the zombie wave never hurt hotdogs.

**The relish bazooka** kills where it hits: a direct hit takes 100 HP, a hotdog's whole health (a stimmed hotdog takes double, as from any rival). Round about, its blast does damage by how far it burst from the nearest point of each body, the body as it is at that moment (`feast::figure`: standing, crouched, prone, or tucked in the air; a cat's too): all of it within 2 cm (`LETHAL`), falling off smoothly (a smoothstep) to nothing at 0.18 m (`SPLASH`, the tuning's `relish.splash`; it was 0.22 m from the chest's middle, falling off in a straight line from 70). About half a hotdog's health mid-way. Solid cover between the burst and the body stops the blast; each light object between (a pillow, a cereal box) takes it down to three quarters (it used to go through them whole). It spares its shooter, who is neither hurt nor thrown, as before. Bots aim it at the feet, so **jumping helps**: a hotdog in the air over a burst at its feet is further from it, and one that holds crouch in the air tucks its knees up (the body shortened from below by what a crouch takes off, the head where the jump put it), further still. **The burst, as it is seen, heard and felt** (`src/arena/splatter/boom.rs`), wherever a glob bursts, whoever fired it (this player, another or a bot, offline or online: the simulation's splat of the glob sets it off, and no other gun's does). A bright core flash; a ball of chunky green relish that swells and bursts at once into dozens of globs and pickle chunks flung out on arcs, with a spray of drops; a green mist billowing up and thinning away over a second and a half; and a **shockwave ring** running out along the counter in a fifth of a second, holding, and fading by three quarters of a second: it marks the **kill zone**, where a hotdog standing on the counter is reached by the blast (its feet within the ring, it is hurt; outside, not: the blast's 0.18 m grown by the body's 0.045 m, met at the body's height, so 0.22 m round a glob bursting on the counter). The ball, the globs and the mist fly further, for show. Large splats are thrown over the counter, the blocks and the walls within the blast, radiating out from it and drawn out away from it on the counter, every block face within reach taking one, dripping down upright faces; the hotdogs and loose objects in it are smeared and marked (loose objects jolt because the simulation's blast throws them); each glob leaves a smaller splat where it lands. They are stains like any other: they dry, and go after 90 s, within the graphics level's cap. Within about the blast's radius of the camera, with nothing solid between, a few green blobs of relish spatter the lens, more and bigger the nearer, and sag, slide down and fade over two seconds or so. The view shakes, hard up close and slightly from across the map. At the High graphics level only, a short green pulse of light. The boom has a wet splat over it and, near the burst, a deep thump: louder and bassier the nearer (`assets/audio/relish-splat.wav`, `relish-thump.wav`, made by `tools/build_relish_sounds.py`). Each graphics level has a budget for one burst and for bursts at once (four at High, three at Medium, two at Low and Lowest; a burst beyond them takes the oldest one's place): High draws up to 110 parts a burst (48 globs) and its light, Medium 76, Low 35 and Lowest 18, and none at Low or Lowest has a light. A burst's parts are made once and kept hidden between bursts, and 2.6 s after it nothing of it runs.

**The ketchup sniper and the mustard pistol in the face.** A sniper round that strikes a hotdog's face kills from full health (`feast::FACE`, a multiplier by gun: 2 for the sniper, 110 HP; 6 for the mustard pistol, 108 HP; 1 for every other gun); in the body the sniper still takes two (55 each) and the pistol six (18 each). A level shot from a standing hotdog's eye at a standing rival facing it meets the face: aim at the chest for the body. The face is the front of the top of the body, where the eyes and the smile are drawn, on the sphere rounds hit a hotdog by: where the round enters it, the band of the eyes and the mouth (rising 0.3 to 0.88 of the sphere's radius, so the top of the bun is body), within 50° either side of the way the hotdog faces, the round coming from in front. It follows the stance (lower crouched, where it stands tucked in the air; prone, the front end of the body lying along the way it faces), so the back of the head and a shot from above the bun are the body. The round goes on through bodies as it always has, a face shot or not. On the victim's record it is the gun's kind with `FACE_SHOT` added (66 for the sniper, 65 for the pistol; `feast::plain_kind` takes it off, so every reader still knows the gun), and the kill feed shows a face in a sight's crosshairs in place of the gun (`Icon::FaceShot`); protocol 12's frames are as they were, and kitchen-ffi reports it as the gun's kind with `KS_EVENT_FLAG_FACE`. **Recoil** goes by how heavy each gun is (the weights above, one table, `crates/kitchen-sim/src/recoil.rs`): in first person the gun kicks back and its muzzle climbs, settling through a spring (it comes back a little past where it rests), and the view is kicked up and a little to a side drawn afresh each shot; the syrup rifle's and the ketchup sniper's kicks are as they were tuned and the relish bazooka's (heaviest, most) is reckoned from them by weight, and the spray guns (the hot sauce thrower, the bug spray, the mayonnaise) push and shake steadily while they are held. In the simulation, so the same for everyone, bots included, each shot also throws the hotdog's aim off a little (its bloom: the next shot is deflected by up to it, mostly up, by a draw from the round's number), the heavier gun more a shot but slowly enough to settle between its shots; a spray gun holds a steady shake instead. **The mustard pistol is a hand cannon**: light in the hand, but its kick is set as it is (`recoil::HAND_CANNON`, 0.018 rad a shot: half as much again as a sniper shot's 0.012, three syrup rounds' worth) and its muzzle takes 0.8 s to come back down, longer than the 0.35 s it takes to fire again. A shot fired as soon as it may goes where the muzzle still is, high (0.7 to all of the bloom up) and a little to a side (up to 0.4 of it), and its kick stacks on what is left, up to five shots' worth (`recoil::FLIP`, 0.09 rad, about 5°): patient shots go true, two quick ones put the second about a centimetre high at 1.2 m, and fired on and on its rounds walk up through the face and over the head. In first person it kicks back, flips up most of the way the sniper does and throws the view up 0.03 rad with a short shake, coming back down over about 0.8 s (a third is left when it can fire again), and a shot fired before then stacks on what is left (`arena.rs` `PISTOL_KICK`). Crouching, prone and aiming down the sights steady it, as they steady the view. The ketchup sniper blows back hard: the gun slams back towards the eye and rears up, the view is thrown up 0.105 rad (6°, three times what it was) and shakes for a moment (the shake is over in about 0.2 s), and all of it has settled in about 0.4 s; the shot, its damage and the hotdog's place are not touched.

**The syrup rifle** is an M16-like battle rifle with a short, chunky optic on its flat-top rail, and it squirts pancake syrup: one pull of the trigger fires a burst of three rounds 0.1 s apart (`feast::VOLLEY_GAP`; the simulation fires the second and third by itself, each along the aim as it is when it leaves: a person's look that step, online the look of the latest command the server has applied, a bot's aim as it tracks), and the next burst can follow 0.20 s after the last round (0.40 s after the first, as before, so the rifle fires as many rounds a second as it did). The burst is spread out in time so that a hotdog walking across has to be tracked through it and led: at its walk (1.25 m/s) it moves 0.125 m between rounds, about the 0.128 m width a round can hit it in, and 0.25 m over the burst, so a burst fired at one spot lands one round (at best two), while one swept with it and led by the rounds' flight (0.07 s at 1.2 m, 0.17 s at 3 m: 9 to 21 cm of its walk) lands all three; against a hotdog standing still all three land. Nine rounds kill (12 each): from the first round to the ninth takes 1.0 s (it was 0.9 s). It holds 60 rounds. Its rounds are amber, glossy and a little stringy; they splat and leave sticky drips. Each round of a burst kicks the gun again before the last kick has settled, so the gun climbs a little through the burst, and the rounds climb too: the second is aimed 0.004 higher (in rise per metre of the aim) than the first and the third 0.008 higher, about 0.8 cm at a metre. Its kick is tight (60% of what it once was) and settles quickly: a little of each round's is left when the next comes, and next to nothing by the next burst. A burst stops when the rifle runs dry, is put away or the hands are full. Its strike (F) is a jab of the rifle butt. On a hotdog's record a syrup round is kind 12.

**The syrup rifle's optic.** The right mouse button puts the rifle's optic to the eye, as it does the sniper's scope: held, to begin with (up while the button is down, down when it is let go), or toggled, one click up and the next down, as the menu's Scope setting says (with neither gun in hand it does nothing; with the hands full, at the card table or dead nothing is aimed, and changing guns puts the aim down). The optic is a short, chunky housing on the rail, seen on the rifle at the hip and in every hotdog's hands: a thick black ring towards the eye round the glass, lugs on the ring at ten, twelve and two o'clock, a mount down to a clamp on the rail with brass thumb nuts, brass-capped turrets, and on the left the knob that lights the reticle, its amber telltale lit (`src/weapons.rs`). The rifle comes up from the hip over 0.15 s until the eye is on the optic's axis 8.7 mm behind its eyepiece: the ring fills the middle of the view (three quarters of its height across), the glass inside it is half the view high, and the receiver sits low under it. Meanwhile the view zooms 2.2 times (the tangent of the field of view shrinks 2.2 times: 85° becomes 45°) and the look is 2.2 times slower, so that the reticle crosses a target as fast as the crosshair does at the hip. It goes back down over 0.15 s. The glass (`src/scope.rs`, `assets/shaders/scope_lens.wgsl`) is dark seen from the hip and clears as it comes to the eye, to a magnified view tinted teal, deeper towards its edge and darker there, with a thin bright rim and a faint reflection that drifts as the view turns; nothing of the rifle behind it ever shows through it. On it, sharp at any size: the **reticle**, a syrup drop whose point is the aim, a thin ring round it broken at its four points (wide enough to frame a hotdog at four metres), two short stadia either side and a post below, lit syrup-amber, turning the colour of the enemy under it (its team's in Forts, its own in Free For All) and closing its ring within the rifle's 4 m, but never for a team mate, whom the rounds pass by, nor for anyone in the siege's wave; and the **readout** in amber seven-segment digits at its lower edge: the range to what the reticle is on in metres and a tenth (the simulation's own distance to a hotdog's body, or to the first block, loose object or counter in the way; dimmed beyond 4 m, dashes with nothing within 12 m), the magazine's count on the left and on the right the burst's three pips, which empty as its rounds go and fill again when the next burst is ready. While the optic is up, the enemy under the reticle glows its colour round its silhouette (a shell over its sausage and buns, added to the picture in the world's pass: whatever stands in front of it hides it; on this screen alone), fading in over 0.1 s and out over 0.15 s. Outside the ring the view is out of focus, by quality level: on Lowest and Low a soft darkening deepening towards the screen's edges, drawn as one quad in the pass the gun already has; on Medium and High a lighter darkening over a blur, two fullscreen passes on the finished picture before tone mapping, sharp within the ring and full a little outside it (`src/scope/blur.rs`), only while the optic is up. The head-up display is drawn over either. Aimed, the kick is half the hip's, and the rounds of a burst climb half as much: 0.002 a round. The ketchup sniper's scope is as it was: the black mask round its crosshair at 18°, the gun put away. Online the aim is a held bit of the player's commands (`button::SCOPE`, the bit after the one that marks a pickup, which a server from before it masks away); the server halves the climb by it and tells every client in the hotdog's flags (bit 13, after everything a client from before it reads), and other players see the hotdog bring the rifle (or the sniper, scoped) up to its right eye. Bots do not aim.

**The mayo squirter** is just a squeeze bottle of mayonnaise, held in the fist: while the trigger is held it squirts a stream, 12 droplets a second (the bottle keeps the time it is owed between steps, so the flow is the same at any frame rate or step). Each droplet leaves the nozzle at 11 m/s and falls at 70 m/s² (25 times the game's gravity: the stream flies five times as fast as it once did, along the arcs it always had, in a fifth of the time), so the stream arcs over, and aiming up throws it further: from a standing hotdog's muzzle it lands 0.68 m off aimed level, 1.40 m at 20°, 1.85 m at 45° (the furthest) and 1.57 m at 60°. It has no reach of its own: a droplet flies until it lands, on the counter, a deck, a ramp, a block, a loose object, a cat or a hotdog, or past the counter's edge on the kitchen floor. Stimmed, the bottle is squeezed harder: 1.4 times as fast out of the nozzle (3.5 m at 45°) and half as many droplets again, 18 a second. A droplet does 5 to a cat and 1.8 to a hotdog, so the stream is steady pressure, and a way of lobbing over cover, not a burst: 60 and 22 a second (90 and 32 stimmed), against the hot sauce's 120 and 40. A bottle holds 150 droplets, 12.5 seconds of squeezing (its magazine: a reload swaps the bottle). It is drawn as one stream (`src/squirt.rs`), not as droplets: between two droplets the stream is what would have left the nozzle in between, so it bends as a sweep of the aim bends a real one, and it squelches for as long as it flows (the squelch, and the mustard pistol's wet squirt, are made by `python3 tools/build_squirt_sounds.py`). Its strike (F) is a bonk with the bottle, brought down bottom first. On a hotdog's record a droplet is kind 13.

**Melee** (F) needs no ammunition and is the same blow whatever delivers it, in every gun mode: it takes 0.48 seconds, reaches 0.21 m along the aim and strikes the nearest formed zombie there, or, where rounds hurt hotdogs (the siege's famine, Free For All, a Forts fight), the nearest hotdog that is no team mate, and shoves it. **The blow depends on where it comes from.** The simulation takes the angle flat, between directly behind the victim (the way opposite to its `yaw`; a zombie's is the way it looks) and the way the attacker stands from it. Inside the rear arc, 60 degrees either side of directly behind (120 degrees in all), the blow is a **backstab** and kills: it does the victim's whole HP, so the death, the kill's credit and the cause all go through the usual path. From anywhere else it takes half of the most HP there is: 50 (half of a hotdog's 100) or 35 (half of a zombie's 70), so a second blow from the front finishes one that has not healed. Facing is the victim's yaw alone: its pitch, crouching or lying prone, what it carries and where the attacker looks make no difference, and a hotdog whose hands are full cannot strike (a loose object, or the bacon). A stimmed victim still takes double from rivals, as it does from everything, so a blow from the front kills it. Bots strike by the same rule, because the rule is the simulation's. The tuning panel's `melee.hotdog` and `melee.zombie` are the blow from the front (50 and 35 by default); a backstab is not tunable and always kills. In the siege's famine alone a living hotdog within 0.17 m is bitten instead, by the same rule, and the bite heals (see [the siege](#the-siege)); Free For All has no bite. A strike never hurts anybody during the wave, nor a team mate in Forts. A backstab is on the victim's record of what last hurt it as kind 14 (BACKSTAB, in place of 6, MELEE, which any other blow leaves), with the attacker, where it stood and the way the blow went: every client already reads that record, so the server's frames, binary and JSON, are as they were (protocol 12).

**The whip.** Each weapon is swung its own way (the mustard pistol is rolled over and hammered down butt first, the ketchup sniper is tipped up and its stock driven forward and down, the hot sauce thrower is held crosswise in both hands and shoved, the bazooka's tube is swung across the body like a bat, the spray can is jabbed, the syrup rifle is swung round level so that its butt is driven in, and the mayo squirter is brought down bottom first), in the same beats for everyone who sees it: a wind-up in which the gun rolls over and lifts (0.10 s), a snap whose butt or grip leads (0.07 s), contact, a follow-through and a smooth recovery, 0.39 s in all (src/whip.rs). When the blow connects the swing **freezes for 0.055 s on contact** (about three frames at 60 Hz; 0.095 s for a backstab), whoever is swinging: the simulation's word, so it is the same for the other players' blows, for bots' and online. In first person the camera leans into the blow, is jolted down when it lands (almost twice as hard for a backstab) and shakes for about a fifth of a second; a smear of light follows the gun along its arc (red for a backstab); a flash and a spray of sauce droplets fly from the victim, with a glint of a slash for a backstab; the swing has its whoosh and the blow its thwack (assets/audio/whoosh.wav, thwack.wav, made by tools/build_melee_sounds.py). The player who killed with a backstab, and the one who was killed by it, see the head-up display's small dagger beside the crosshair for a second and a half: an icon, no words.

**The turret** turns on its post, at up to 2 radians a second, to track the nearest formed zombie it can see within 1.4 m of its muzzle, and once it points at it pulses an instant beam at it five times a second for 4 damage. It fires at nothing else: a hotdog standing in the beam is neither hurt nor pushed, and a zombie that is still forming is not a target. With no zombie to track it sweeps slowly, 0.6 radians either side of the yaw the map gave it, through the warm-up and the free for all. Its kills count for the wave and for nobody's score.

**Crouch** (hold Ctrl or C) is the stance between standing and prone: the body is 0.10 m tall instead of 0.15 m, the muzzle drops from 0.135 m to 0.09 m and the middle of what a round can hit from 0.105 m to 0.055 m (so level mustard, ketchup and hot sauce from a standing hotdog pass over it; a relish glob does not), it walks at 65% speed, recoil is three quarters, and it can still jump. **Held in the air it tucks the knees up** (`Actor::tuck`): the body is shortened from below instead, the feet drawn up towards it, the head, the muzzle, the eye and the middle of what a round hits staying where the jump put them (the first-person view does not dip); the feet keep the jump's arc, so no height is gained and nothing cleared that a plain jump would not clear, and it lands crouched. Seen from outside the legs fold up under the body. It is what takes a relish glob at the feet down to a graze. **Prone** lowers the hotdog below level fire, lowers its muzzle, slows it to 40% speed, halves recoil, and rules out jumping (Space stands up); prone wins over crouch. **Stim** lasts 6 seconds with a 20-second cooldown: 150% move speed, higher jumps (0.19 m instead of 0.11 m), 40% recoil, and double damage taken from other hotdogs (zombies, pests, the turret and hunger are unchanged). While it lasts the hotdog is wrapped from the top of its head to its feet in flickering blue fire, its tongues licking upwards, with short jagged arcs of lightning crawling over it and leaping to its hands and feet, and it lights the counter blue round it (no light at the Low and Lowest graphics levels, and fewer arcs). In first person the same fire licks up round the bottom and the sides of the view, leaving the crosshair clear, and lightning crackles over the hands and the gun. It all comes up with the clap and dies away over the stim's last half second (`src/player_model/stim.rs`).

**Camping** summons flies and parasites, in the siege only (its wave and its famine). A hotdog that stays within 0.25 m of one spot for 10 seconds there draws a swarm over an area the size of the turret deck (0.51 m × 0.45 m). It stings everyone inside for 6 HP a second and leaves once the area has been empty for 4 seconds, or after five puffs of bug spray.

### Materials and rounds

Everything a round can meet is solid or light, in every mode (`crates/kitchen-sim/src/props.rs`: `MATERIALS` gives each kind of loose object its material, one line to a kind, and a kind not listed is solid):

| Material | What | A round that reaches it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Solid | the map's blocks and walls, the forcefield, a draped napkin's wall; a chair, a crate, the bowl, the tray, a napkin (lying flat or draped), a mug, a plate, a cereal bowl, a tin, a cutting board, a salt or pepper shaker, the bread bin | stops there, whatever the gun (the sniper's too); a glob's splash does not reach past it |
| Light | a pillow, a cereal box, a roll of paper towels | goes through, left 0.6 of its speed and 0.75 of its damage (and of its splash) for each light object it passes, and splats where it is once that leaves it slower than 1.5 m/s (0.3 m/s when rounds were five times slower); a glob's splash goes through it |

So a mustard round gets through three pillows in a row and stalls in the fourth, and a relish glob, slower, bursts in the third. A round is not slowed twice by one pillow, and it gives the pillow a small push on its way through. Streams follow the same rule: the hot sauce's droplets, the bug spray's mist and the mayonnaise's droplets (each of which sets out on a new, slower arc from the pillow). A blow (F) and the turret's beam are not rounds: any loose object stops them. Bots shoot through a pillow as a round goes through one (see [bots](#bots)).

**The ketchup sniper's round goes through bodies**, in every mode: it hurts every hotdog and formed cat it passes, each once and at its full damage (less what pillows took), and flies on until its 6 m run out or something solid stops it, so two rivals in a line both take 55, and two cats in a line both die. Each body it hits is splatted where the round went in, is on its own record as hit by the sniper from the shooter's muzzle along the shot, and counts to the shooter, so a death by a round that went on is read as any other (the client's death camera reads the record). Every other gun's round ends at the first body it meets.

### Pickups

A map's pickups are spawn points. Each stands on a pad: a dark disc ringed by 24 lights, lit all round, amber for a gun and green for a box of ammunition, while its pickup is there. A pickup taken comes back to its pad **20 seconds** later if it is a gun and **12 seconds** later if it is a box of ammunition, and a power weapon (the ketchup sniper, the relish bazooka) between **30 and 60 seconds** later, drawn each time: the game's rules, the same in every gun mode and on every map (`magazine::COMEBACK`, `POWER_BACK`; a map's own `respawn` is read and passed over). Meanwhile its pad stays on the counter, dark, and its ring lights again a segment at a time, the next one blinking, as the pickup's return draws near, and standing on it the head-up display shows, under the crosshair, the pickup's icon and the seconds until it is back. It grows back to its size when it returns. In Forts the pickups are on their pads from the start of the build phase, and each side takes only those on its own side while the forcefield stands (see [Forts](#forts)).

- **What a gun's pickup gives** is the game's rules', whatever the map says (`magazine::PICKUP`; a map's own `ammo` is read and passed over): a magazine (12 mustard, 90 hot sauce, 40 bug spray, 60 syrup, 150 mayonnaise), and the power weapons fewer, **4 ketchup and 2 relish**.
- **A gun** the hotdog does not hold is taken with E: the gun, its rounds filling its magazine first and the rest going to its reserve; it takes the place of the one in hand when the hotdog already holds two (see [weapons](#weapons)). **A gun it holds** is not E's: walking over its pickup puts its rounds into that gun's reserve, as many as there is room for, with no key, and the head-up display pops up the box's icon and +N. A hotdog whose reserve for that gun is full leaves it where it is.
- **A box of ammunition** (an olive crate with three brass rounds in it) is taken by walking over it, with no key: it tops up the reserve of every gun the hotdog holds to the most it carries (four magazines of mustard, 48; three of ketchup, 18, and of syrup, 180; two of everything else: 180 hot sauce, 8 relish, 80 bug spray, 300 mayonnaise: `magazine::CARRY`). No gun is given, and the magazines are as they were (R loads them). A hotdog whose reserves are all full leaves the box where it is, for somebody else. A box never tops up the ketchup sniper or the relish bazooka, in any mode. Every siege map has two, either side of its middle.
- **Whoever reaches a pickup first has it**: the commands as the server takes them (E), then the hotdogs walking over pads, by number, then the bots' errands. The power weapons' pickups give their few rounds (4 ketchup, 2 relish) to whoever reaches them first, walked over by a hotdog that holds one already.

### Bodies

Every living hotdog and every formed cat has a solid body: an upright capsule 0.15 m tall (0.10 m crouching, 0.06 m prone), as wide as the bun for a hotdog (0.045 m in radius) and 0.03 m in radius for a cat, moved by one set of rules (`crates/kitchen-sim/src/actor.rs`, which single player, the client's prediction and the server all run). Two hotdogs touching are 0.09 m apart, a hotdog and a cat 0.075 m.

- **The level.** Walking obeys the map exactly as before (every body keeps the map's 0.03 m margin from blocks, whatever its width), and a hotdog that walks into a wall diagonally slides along it. One gravity (2.8 m/s²) acts on everybody: a jump is a parabola, and walking off the deck or the side of a ramp is a fall, 0.45 s from the deck, for bots and cats as well. A jump keeps the walk's pace through the air (0.57 s aloft on the level, 0.71 m at a walk, 1.40 m stimmed; it was 0.33 and 0.64 m when the hotdogs walked at 0.57 m/s): no higher than before, so no ledge, block or wall is cleared that was not, but further, so a stimmed hotdog can now jump from Counter Siege's turret deck onto the steel block nearest it, out of the cats' reach until camping brings the pests.
- **Each other.** Bodies cannot overlap or pass through one another. A hotdog walking into another, or into a cat, pushes it and is slowed in proportion to their masses (a hotdog weighs 0.4 kg, a cat 0.3 kg). Nobody is pushed into a block or over the counter's edge: a body with a wall behind it does not give way, and whoever is pushing steps round it or stops. Whoever comes down on another lands on its head and slides off; only a stim jump clears a standing body (a plain jump comes down on a crouching one's head). Cats no longer stack, and hotdogs that share a start are parted.
- **Knockback.** A relish glob throws every body within 0.30 m of where it bursts (about 1 m/s for a hotdog at the centre, fading to nothing at the edge, cover shielding as it does from the damage). It throws cats always and hotdogs once rounds are hostile, never the shooter. Other rounds and the melee strike nudge what they hit, in proportion to the damage. A thrown body flies, lands, slides to rest under friction, and until then its player has a quarter of the usual control and cannot jump.
- **The counter's edge** is a wall. `bodies.config.open_edge` makes it open to thrown bodies, which then fall to the kitchen floor and die; nothing switches it on yet.

Dead hotdogs and cats that are still forming have no body: a corpse can be walked over and eaten, and a puddle neither blocks nor is pushed. A cat that finishes forming under somebody pushes them aside.

### Zombie alley cats

The wave arrives through the microwave. Its clock counts down the wave timer; at zero it dings, the door swings open, and a thick ooze brims over the sill, runs down the front and flows across the counter (0.5 m/s, around blocks) to the map's zombie spawn points. On a map with several microwaves each spawn point is fed by the nearest one; on a map with none the ooze wells up out of the counter at the spawn points. Each puddle collects for 1.5 seconds, then rises and shapes itself into zombie alley cats, 1.8 seconds apiece, one cat every 1.2 seconds across the puddles. On the bundled map the first cat is on its feet 5 seconds after the ding and the sixth 11 seconds after it.

**The wave is as big as the map makes it:** two cats for every zombie spawn point, and never fewer than six, so the bundled map's three points bring six and a map with the editor's limit of 64 brings 128. Every spawn point forms its share. However many they are, the cats of one arrival all begin to form within the 7.2 seconds the six of the bundled map take, each once its own puddle has pooled: more than six share that time evenly (24 begin 0.3 s apart, 128 begin 0.056 s apart), so a big wave is a horde on its feet together, not a queue. A puddle still forms one cat at a time and rests 0.6 seconds between two. With 64 points scattered over the bundled counter the last of 128 is on its feet about 17 seconds after the ding. An arrival of fewer cats than the map has spawn points (`emerge(n)` with a small `n`) takes points spread evenly through the map's list, and each such arrival starts one point further on, so no spawn point is left out for good.

The wave ends when every cat is dead, and the free for all begins. A wave that is too much for the hotdogs ends the round instead: once fewer than two hotdogs are left alive the last one standing has won (or nobody has), as in the free for all. As the bots play now, the demo's five clear the 64 cats of 32 spawn points scattered over the bundled counter with four of them left, and are overrun by 80 or more: 128 cats leave one hotdog standing about 17 seconds after the ding.

A horde is drawn and stepped as cheaply as six cats were. Each cat is sixteen meshes (a trunk, a head, a jaw, a tail in three pieces, thighs, shins, the eyes), sculpted once for each of the five coats and shared, with a handful of shared materials: 128 cats are about 2,300 meshes (a primitive for every lump, tooth and claw was 12,000, and a blob for every bead of 64 flows 8,000 more). A cat is 15,700 triangles; with more than 40 cats about, those further than 0.9 m from the eye wear a coarser coat of 5,400, so a horde seen from across the counter is under a million triangles and one all round the camera at most 2.3 million. Everything the ooze does on the counter (flows, puddles, bubbles) is one mesh for each arrival, reshaped every frame. A cat that has to search for its way round a block keeps to the waypoint it found for up to nine more steps, and one that has no way to its hotdog at all (walled off) does not search for one.

A cat that is still forming cannot be hurt, and rounds fly straight through it; it does not move or bite, and bots hold their fire until it has formed. A formed cat prowls at 0.22 m/s (the hotdogs' walk times 0.18, as it was beside their old walk) to a place of its own in a ring round the nearest hotdog, 0.10 m out, going round the back of the ring to reach it; there it stops, faces the hotdog and lunges. It bites within 0.125 m, so the eight cats of a full ring all bite, and any more wait one rank out for a place. It climbs the ramps after a hotdog on the deck, and melts back into ooze when it dies. The simulation owns the whole arrival: `Feast::emerge(n)` (the server runs the same `Feast`) starts an emergence of `n` zombies, and the wave is one such call.

Teammates cannot damage each other during the zombie wave. The famine enables player damage. Its eating and its bite are [the siege's](#the-siege). Dead players stop moving and fighting. Round replay resets health, food, enemies, weapons, and player bodies.

### Bots

The hotdogs nobody is playing use whatever they hold, alone, in the demo and on the server alike:

- **Shopping**, the same in every gun mode. With a hand free a bot walks to the nearest gun it lacks that no other bot is after and collects it (bug spray counts as a metre further off). It holds the mustard pistol it starts with as it would a free hand: it trades it for the first gun it collects, keeping its rifle (for bug spray only while its other gun is loaded). It goes back for ammunition when a gun runs low (to a pickup of that gun, or to a box of ammunition while a gun it holds that a box tops up, not a power weapon, is below half its full load), and swaps an empty gun for a new one (not for bug spray); with two loaded guns and no pistol it wants no other gun. It shops during the warm-up and while the ooze is still spreading, and otherwise only when no zombie or rival is close (or its guns are all empty); a bot whose guns are all below a quarter of their load goes further for one. It knows pickups come back: one that has been taken it sets out for if it will be back by the time it gets there (at its walking pace), and waits on the pad if it is early. A pickup it cannot reach it gives up on.
- **Range**, the same in every gun mode. It fires the syrup rifle in bursts at anything within 3.8 m (it prefers it to every other gun there but the hot sauce up close, a relish glob with its burst clear, and the ketchup sniper beyond 0.8 m), keeps it in hand beyond, and turns to its other gun once the rifle is empty; it fires the ketchup sniper at targets more than 0.8 m off (one round kills a zombie) and backs away from anything that comes within 0.7 m; carries the hot sauce thrower in from up to 1.2 m and sprays inside 0.6 m; fires a relish glob only at a target more than 0.6 m away; squirts the mayonnaise along its arc (the low one, or lobbed over on the high one where cover is in the way of the low one, or of its other gun) at anything within its throw, walking in until it is, and keeping to the middle of its throw from a rival; and uses the mustard pistol otherwise. Rounds are led by what the bot has seen of the target's pace, as a person leads (see **Aim**). When one gun is empty the next shot comes from the other. What stops its shot is what is solid: a bot shoots through a pillow, a cereal box or a roll of paper towels as a round goes through one, and holds its fire for a chair, a crate, a napkin, a cutting board or anything else solid in the way.
- **Aim** (`crates/kitchen-sim/src/aim.rs`). A bot aims as a person does, not as a machine, in every mode where bots shoot (Free For All, the siege's wave and famine, Forts; not the turrets, which are not bots, nor Kung Fu's fighters). It aims where it thinks its target is, which lags a moment behind where it is, and leads it by the pace it has seen it move at, over or under by a share drawn each shot. Each shot leaves off its aim by an error drawn from a cone that is wider at a moving target, at a longer range, while the bot itself walks, just after it has been hit, and for the first shots at a target, narrowing as it keeps on the same one (it walks its shots in). It does not fire at a target it has just seen until it has reacted (0.35 to 0.6 s at normal skill), and after each shot it takes a moment to bring its aim back onto the target (0.4 to 0.8 s on top of the gun's own time between shots; not for the spray guns' streams). Through a syrup burst it keeps tracking, with its lag, each round leaving where it aims then, its hand wandering a little between rounds: at a target strafing across it lands one or two of a burst more often than all three. The ketchup sniper's scope takes a moment longer to settle and misses a moving target more; the spray guns are forgiving as they are; the relish bazooka is aimed at the feet. With the mustard pistol (a hand cannon) a normal or hard bot waits for its muzzle to come back down before it fires again; an easy one does not, and fires sooner, so its rounds walk up; a hard bot sometimes goes for the face with it, as with the sniper. The guns' own bloom and climb come on top of it, as for everyone. Every draw comes from the game's seed, so the same game aims the same way. A bot that sees a relish glob coming at it may jump it, tucking its knees up (more often the better it is). **Skill**: `skill=easy|normal|hard` in the rules (the page's address, `?play&skill=hard`, or the sandbox's; `bots=easy` also does), normal unless asked. At a still target 1.2 m off a normal bot lands about 59% of its syrup rifle's rounds, at one strafing across at a walk (1.25 m/s) about 31%, and the ketchup sniper about 25% at one strafing; easy about 0.4 to 0.55 of those, hard about 1.3 to 1.45 times, short of perfect. A person standing still in a Free For All with four normal bots lasts about 6 s on average once a bot first sees it (it was 3.2 s before the bots aimed as people do), and in the siege's famine about 3.6 s. `skill=perfect` is the machine's aim the bots once had, for tests and trying things out. Online, `hotdog-server --bots easy|normal|hard` plays every lobby game at that skill (a sandbox's own `skill=` stands); the lobby has no control for it.
- **Relish.** During the wave it fires no glob whose burst could reach another hotdog wherever along its way it lands, allowing for how far a hotdog can walk while it flies; it keeps out of its own burst always.
- **Melee.** It strikes with what is in its hand when a zombie is within 0.17 m, and closes in to strike when every gun is empty and there is no ammunition to fetch. In Free For All it strikes a rival within 0.15 m, and closes in to strike when its guns are empty; in the siege's famine it bites instead (and eats a corpse when it is under 80 HP), and charges to bite when its HP is under 50. It strikes by the simulation's rule like everyone (a kill from behind its target, half HP from in front), but it does not go round to get behind anything.
- **Reloading.** A bot reloads a magazine at once when it runs dry, a half-empty one when its target is out of the gun's reach, and any that is not full while nothing is about; while it reloads it goes about its business without firing, and taking its other gun in hand (when that one suits the range better) cancels it. A gun run dry altogether it gives up for its other gun, or a pickup, or its teeth: with nothing left it fetches rounds when there are any to be had, and otherwise closes in to strike or bite.
- **Pests.** A bot with the can sprays the swarm that is on it where it stands (five puffs); one without steps out of it.

## Basics (every gun mode)

The siege (its wave and its famine), Forts (its build and its fight) and Free For All play by the same basics (`crates/kitchen-sim/src/rules.rs`, `magazine.rs`); each mode adds only its objective (below, and [the siege](#the-siege), [Forts](#forts), [Free For All](#free-for-all-and-the-sandbox)):

- **One loadout, for people and bots alike**: the syrup rifle in hand, 60 in its magazine and 180 in reserve, and the mustard pistol beside it, 12 and 48 (`magazine::loadout`). Free For All's respawn restores it.
- **Rounds run out** in every mode: more comes only from the pickups and the boxes (the sandbox's `ammo=inf`, and `mode=ffa ammo=inf`, make them unlimited).
- **The pickups are the rules', not the map's**: a gun's pickup gives a magazine, and comes back 20 s after it is taken (a box of ammunition 12 s); see [pickups](#pickups).
- **The power weapons are scarce**: a ketchup sniper's pickup gives 4 rounds and a relish bazooka's 2, a box of ammunition tops up neither, and one taken comes back 30 to 60 s later, drawn from the game's seed (`Feast::comeback`). The first to grab one has it.
- **F is the strike** along the aim: a kill from behind, half of a hotdog's health from in front, at whoever the mode lets a hotdog hurt (see [melee](#weapons)). The healing bite and eating corpses are the siege's famine's alone.
- **A hotdog killed scores** its killer a kill and 100 points, in every mode (a cat 100; a bite or a meal 25 on top, in the famine).
- **The bots** choose, trade and fire their guns the same way in every mode (see [bots](#bots)).
- **The head nods with the look** (`crates/kitchen-sim/src/head.rs`): looking down bows the top of the hotdog up to 45° forward, its neck curving, and looking up tips it 25° back (prone, 15° down at the most); others, bots and replays show it, eased (`src/player_model/bend.rs`), and the first-person eye stays where it was. Rounds find the head where the nod has it: a hotdog looking down is lower and shorter from behind (a round at the top of a level head passes over it), its face turned down (easier from below, harder from straight ahead), while the body below the neck is hit as it always was. Kung Fu's poses keep the head as they had it.

What is left to each mode is its objective: the wave's cats and its rule that no round hurts a hotdog, the famine's hunger, pests, bite and eating; Forts' teams (team mates spared), its build phase (guns locked, each side's own pickups, R as Ready), the bacon and its carrier's slower pace; Free For All's respawns and spawn protection where the others eliminate; and each mode's end and top bar. How big a siege's wave is stays the map's: two cats for each of its cat spawn points (Horde Night's 18 make it a horde).

## The siege

Five hotdogs. A 12-second warm-up, then **the wave**: the zombie cats come out of the microwave ([zombie alley cats](#zombie-alley-cats)); nobody's rounds, blasts or blows hurt a hotdog while it lasts, and a swarm of pests stings whoever camps. When the last cat dies **the famine** begins: everybody against everybody, HP drains from hunger (2.2 a second), pests still sting campers, and F with a living hotdog within 0.17 m of the feet is **the bite**, the melee blow (a kill from behind, half its HP from the front) that heals the biter 80% of the first 24 HP it takes; **E eats a corpse** for 38 HP, once. The last hotdog standing wins; nobody comes back within the round. Any map but the dojo can be played as a siege: `?play&mode=siege&map=pillow-fort` plays a Forts map so (its zones passed over).

## Forts

In multiplayer the lobby's leader picks Forts and a Forts map (see [the lobby](#the-lobby)); a server can also start on it:

```sh
crates/server/target/release/hotdog-server --mode forts     # multiplayer, on pillow-fort until the leader chooses otherwise
```

or <http://localhost:8137/?play&map=pillow-fort> to play it alone with bots on both teams, <http://localhost:8137/?demo&map=pillow-fort> to watch six bots, and natively `--skip-intro --map assets/maps/pillow-fort.json` (with `--demo` to watch). The mode is the game's: Forts plays on any map with the Forts layout, a base for each team, a field and the bacon's spot (`?mode=forts`, the lobby, `--mode forts`), and a map made for Forts plays it when nothing else says; a Forts map can be played as a siege or Free For All too (its zones passed over). The lobby does not offer Forts on a map without the layout, and says why.

**Teams.** The Mustard Eaters and the Ketchup Connoisseurs, three hotdogs each (six in all, where the siege has five), with a base at each end of the map. In multiplayer each player picks a team in the lobby, or leaves it to the server; a side holds three, so a fourth who asks for it is put on the other, those who did not ask go to the smaller side, and bots make both up to three. (The lobby still holds five people, so there is always at least one bot.) Alone, the player is a Mustard Eater. A hotdog wears its team's colour (its legs, its badge, a band round its head and a block of the colour on a pole at its back), its name and HP float over it in that colour (see [the head-up display](#the-head-up-display)), and the score is at the top of the screen in the middle. Team mates cannot hurt each other: their rounds fly on past, their bursts neither hurt nor throw, and their blows do not land.

**A round** has no respawns, and goes:

1. **Build**, for the game's build time: 150 seconds, a rule (the server's `--build S`, `?build=S` in a browser or `--build S` natively replace it; the demo builds for 25; a map's own `build` is read and passed over). A blue forcefield stands right across the map: it stops hotdogs, loose objects, rounds and routes exactly as a block does (in the simulation it is a block, 3 m high, for as long as it stands), and nobody can be hurt by anything. **Nobody can fire or strike while it stands**: the trigger and F do nothing with a gun in hand (the first-person gun is held lowered, muzzle down, and the head-up display puts a lock on the gun's icon, which flashes red when the trigger is pressed), and bots do not shoot; the trigger still throws a carried object, which is building, not a weapon. Each side has its stock of loose objects to carry about and build with: only what a kitchen counter has, at its true size beside a 15 cm hotdog (see [loose objects](#loose-objects)), set out tidily clear of the bases, the pads, the starts and the ways from the bases to the bacon. On the bundled map each side has three cutting boards in a pile by the front of its base, a bread bin in the back corner with two tins on it, two napkins folded flat, a row of four cereal boxes along the front edge, two more tins, a pile of five plates with a cereal bowl on top, two rolls of paper towels by the microwave, another bowl, three mugs and two salt shakers and a pepper shaker: 58 loose objects in all (the jungle and moonlit forts 56, a row of three cereal boxes a side; the whole kitchen's 56). What stands in a pile is locked as it is put out (see [carrying](#carrying)), so the piles stand tidy, cost nothing and are taken from the top. **Nothing may be put down in a base** (see [carrying](#carrying)). The weapon pickups are on their pads from the start, and each side can take those on its own side of the field (a gun taken comes to hand, locked like the rest until the field drops; bots take one too, if they want it); the bacon cannot be touched. The countdown is on the screen. When every living hotdog that a person plays has pressed R (ready; again to take it back), the field drops early; bots are not asked.
2. **Fight.** The forcefield sinks into the counter with a thud, the guns are unlocked and both sides' pickups can be reached by either (on the bundled map each team has a ketchup sniper, a hot sauce thrower, a relish bazooka and two lots of mustard on its own side of the middle, a syrup rifle up on its back deck and a box of ammunition at the front corner of its base). They are spawn points, as in the siege: one taken comes back to its pad 20 seconds later (a box of ammunition 12 seconds, a power weapon 30 to 60: see below), counted back on its ring (see [pickups](#pickups)); one taken during the build is not hurried back by the field's drop. The bacon lies on its plate in the middle. Whoever touches it (within 0.12 m) carries it, on its back where everybody can see it, and lets go of whatever else it carried; it uses no weapon at all (neither its trigger nor F does anything) and can only run, jump, crouch and take its stim, at 55% of its pace (`forts::BACON_PACE`, 0.75 until the bots' bursts went: at that pace nearly every bot round was a capture some seven seconds after the field dropped; now about half are, the rest wipes). Bots carrying the bacon run home and stim when anything comes within 1.2 m. A carrier standing in **its own** base has won the round for its team; the other base does nothing. A carrier who dies drops the bacon where it fell, anyone can pick it up, and if nobody does for 6 seconds it goes back to the middle. A team with nobody left alive has lost the round (both at once: nobody's point). The dead stay dead until the round is over.
3. **Stalemate.** If 45 seconds of a fight pass without a hotdog dying and without the bacon being picked up, the microwave goes off and lets out zombie cats, by the usual ooze: 2 the first time, 2 more each time it happens again in the round (4, 6, ...; never more than 12 at once). The cats go for whoever is nearest, of either team, and bite as in the siege. `--stale S` on the server changes the 45 seconds.
4. **Round over**: the score is shown for 8 seconds, then the next round begins with a new build phase and every loose object back where the map has it. **The first team to three rounds wins the match**; in multiplayer everyone then returns to the lobby (where sides can be changed), and alone or in the demo a new match starts.

There is no opening wave, no free for all, no hunger, no eating and no pests in Forts. The loadout, the pickups (the power weapons' scarcity among them), F and the score for a kill are the [basics](#basics-every-gun-mode) of every gun mode. A gun it does not hold takes the place of the one in hand (whose rounds are lost), so picking up a sniper with the rifle in hand gives up the rifle; take the pistol in hand first (2) to keep the rifle. A power weapon that has run dry stays in hand, empty, as any gun does, until it is swapped for another or a pickup of its own refills it. A turret, if the map has one, fires at zombies only, as always.

**Dead**, a player watches its own body from the death camera for four seconds and is then shown a living team mate from behind; a click (or Space) shows the next one, and the mouse and the wheel take the camera round and nearer or further off as they do in the siege (see [the head-up display](#the-head-up-display)).

**Bots build forts to blueprints.** At the start of each build phase each team's bots choose a fort design (`forts::blueprint::DESIGNS`), at random from the game's seed, the round and the team among those that fit the map well, drawn plain or mirrored across the base, and the other team's drawn mirrored across the field. Every design rings or fronts its base, outside it, and leaves a gate, a clear way on foot from the field into the base for the bacon:

- **the keep**: a front wall of two cutting boards stood on their long edges with a gate between them, a roll of paper towels either side of the gate with a cereal bowl on each, cereal boxes standing as buttresses at the ends, a board along one side of the base and the bread bin along the other with tins and a mug beside it under a napkin roof, and out in front a palisade of plates on their rims, a napkin hung over each half;
- **the gatehouse**: a covered way in between two boards stood out from the base's front, with towers of paper towel rolls and bowls at its mouth, low walls of cereal boxes on their sides either side crowned with tins, mugs and shakers, the bread bin as a roofed bastion at one end and plates on their rims at the other, under a napkin;
- **the ravelin**: a front wall of two boards with a gate, buttresses at its ends, a third board out in front as a screen that the way in goes round, roll towers with bowls at its ends, two roofed bastions at the corners (the bread bin with tins, a cereal box with mugs), and plates and shakers as low cover.

Each place of a design is checked against the map (the counter's edges, its blocks, decks and ramps, the forcefield, both bases, the pickups' pads, the spawn points, the bacon's spot, and room to stand to build it), and what does not fit is left out. A bot builds with the real actions, as a player would: it walks to a piece (the nearest of the kind the place wants, a cutting board for a wall panel, the bread bin for a block, a roll for a post, a plate for a shield, a napkin for a roof; when none of that kind is left, one of much the same size; any small piece for a topper), picks it up, turns and tips it with the wheel's and X's actions, carries it to where it must stand, stops, looks at where it would land (the same preview a player sees) and puts it down, so that it snaps and locks as anybody's does; off its place, it steps over and tries again, and a place whose piece will not go, or is knocked off, is tried again a few times. Foundations and walls come first, then posts and outworks, then what goes on top, then the roofs. The bots of a team spread over the places and the pieces, never cross the forcefield and never put anything in a base. A piece lying where a place goes is taken off it first (and a pile taken from the top, the bowl off the plates before a plate), a piece with no place to go is set down in the team's yard, clear of the fort and its gate, and a piece is never put where it would shut away a piece still wanted (that piece is carried out first). The plan holds only what the bots can finish: a kind stands in for another only where it fits, a roof goes where it rests steadily on what it covers (so that it locks there rather than tipping off), and a place that would close the bots' own way out of the fort is left out; a place none of whose kinds is left is given up. A napkin set aside goes on bare counter (or on napkins lying flat), never over other pieces, where it would drape and shut them in until the fight, and a bot goes only for pieces it can walk to. A bot that wants a gun or a box of ammunition on its own side fetches it first, as in the fight (as everywhere, it trades its pistol for the first gun it reaches). Bots find their way round the pieces standing on the counter, so that they go in and out of the forts by the gates, in the build and in the fight.

**In the fight**, two of a team's three go for the bacon, or for whoever carries it (to guard a team mate, to stop a rival), and the third stands guard inside its own fort, behind the wall by the gate, while all three live (whatever a bot still holds in the fight it puts down; in its base, where nothing may be put, it first walks out by its fort's gate); a bot with the bacon runs home, in by the gate; all of them fetch, choose and fire guns as bots do in every gun mode (see [bots](#bots)): the nearest rival or cat they can see, with the gun that suits the range, whenever they are aimed and ready. A bot that has got nowhere for a second jumps and steps aside; one near the bacon that the pieces lying round it have cut off from it walks straight at it, shoving them. A bot low on rounds goes further for a pickup, a box of ammunition above all. **A bot that draped walls have cut off** in the fight (there is no way on foot round the walls to where it is going, the bacon, its carrier or its guard post, though there would be without them) walks up to the nearest wall, pulls the napkin off and lays it down behind itself, away from the wall (to one side, where behind it would be in a base), so that it does not drape there again, and goes on; a bot that can go round a wall leaves it standing, and bots never pull a wall otherwise (in particular never their own team's in the build).

### Carrying

A hotdog can carry one loose object, in the siege as well as in Forts. Q picks up the one it looks at within 0.30 m of its muzzle, or failing that the nearest whose middle is within 0.24 m of its feet. The object is held in front of the hotdog, a little above its feet, turns with it, and collides with nothing while carried (though a round stops at it as at any solid loose object, so a chair or a crate held is a shield of sorts; a pillow held is not). The guns are put away meanwhile and the hotdog cannot strike. The heavier the load, the slower its carrier (`feast::burden_of`): under 0.2 kg (a pillow, a napkin, a roll of paper towels, a shaker) it walks at its full pace; up to 0.6 kg (a crate, a chair, the bowl, the tray, a mug, a plate, a tin) at 80%; heavier at 80% times the square root of 0.6 kg over the load, never under 55% (a cereal box 77%, the bread bin and a cutting board 55%). A hotdog carrying something holds both hands out in front of it. In first person the object is drawn at its true size, as everybody else sees it, where it is held and turned as it is, so a crate or a chair hides a good part of the view below the crosshair; only where its top would stand within 7° of the line of sight (looking level) is it drawn lower, just far enough to keep the crosshair and what it is aimed at in view (a pillow and the tray are held low enough already; held, a crate's top is above the eye and is let down 3.5 cm, the bowl 7 cm, and a chair, whose back stands taller than the hotdog, 10 cm). It sways a little behind a turn and with the stride (the bob as deep at a walk as it ever was, quicker the faster the walk), and it is drawn over the world, as a gun is, so it never sinks into a wall in front or into the counter where it is drawn lower (`loose::held_place`). A napkin's cloth hangs from two fists low in the view (see [loose objects](#loose-objects)), and everybody else sees it hanging from the carrier's hands.

**Turning and tipping what is carried.** While it carries a loose object, the **mouse wheel** turns it about the vertical an eighth of a turn (45°) a notch, rolled down clockwise seen from above and up the other way, and **X** tips it a quarter turn: over onto its front (a cutting board stands on its long edge, a 45 cm by 30 cm wall panel; a cereal box lies on its back; a plate stands on its rim; a tin, a mug or a roll lies on its side), then onto its side (a board stands on end, 45 cm tall; a cereal box lies on its side), then back as the map stood it. A napkin, which is cloth, is turned but never tipped. It is held, previewed and put down as it is turned and tipped, everything in the put below worked out from its outline held so, and a neighbour stood on its edge or on end is as square to snap to as one lying flat. Picked up, anything is held as the map stood it. Online the turn and the tip go to the server in every command (`button::TURN`, `button::TIP`: five bits above `button::SCOPE` that a server from before them masks away), so it lands there as it is held here (`kitchen_sim::feast::build::Pose`).

- **Q again puts it down** (`crates/kitchen-sim/src/build.rs`). Standing still (the hotdog's pace under 0.12 m/s, its feet on something) it lands where it is held, the carrying distance in front, snapped to what is there:
  - flush on the highest thing under the middle of it (3 cm in from its edges: the counter, a deck, a block low enough to reach, or a loose object), and on a loose object turned square to it and drawn into line with it, middle to middle (or edge to edge) when it is within 3 cm of that: that is how a column or a wall is stacked;
  - with no loose object under it, turned square to a neighbour it is put beside, or else to the nearest quarter turn of the hotdog's facing;
  - flush against a neighbour's side within 2 cm of its own (another loose object at the same height, a block, the forcefield), half a millimetre apart, and lined up with it along that side within 3 cm; out of a block's side, or of something that would be under one side of it only, however far in it was held.
  - There it is **locked**, if its middle has something under it: walking hotdogs cannot shove it, nothing slides or topples it, and rounds meet it by its material as ever. It comes loose when it is picked up, when a relish glob's blast throws it, or when something it stands on is taken away or moved, and then it falls. One whose middle has nothing under it (a cutting board across a gap) is let go and locks where it comes to rest.

  Where the hotdog looks does not matter, so standing still it lands in the same spot every time. On the move it is let go where it is held with the hotdog's own pace (averaged over a tenth of a second, at most 1.1 m/s), and in the air (a jump) with its rising or falling speed too, and the physics says where it goes; it locks where it comes to rest. `Feast::placement` says where it would land, turned how, what it would snap to, whether it would be locked, for a napkin whether it would drape and over what, and whether it may be put there at all; it changes nothing and costs little, and `Feast::put` lands it exactly there. (A chair put down used to be thrown: it was set on the highest thing under any of five points of its outline, its corners included, so beside a crate, or turned a little on one, it went onto the crate's top with most of it in the air, tipped and fell.)
- **The placement preview** (`src/preview.rs`) shows that landing every frame while the player carries something: a see-through **ghost** of the object (its own shape, unlit, its rim and rounded edges glowing, casting no shadow) where Q would put it down and turned as it would land, with a thin box round it. Its colour says what Q would do:

  | Colour | Meaning |
  | --- | --- |
  | white | a plain put: on the move it is let go with the hotdog's pace and settles where the physics takes it (locking there) |
  | cyan | it snaps onto something or flush beside it, but settles before it locks |
  | green | it is **locked** where it lands |

  Whatever it snaps to (the object it lands on, the one it is drawn flush against) glows cyan, and the tooltip says the same in words (`Q: put down (snaps, locks)`, see [hints](#hints)). Where it may not be put down (in a Forts base, below) the ghost is **grey**, nothing glows, the tooltip says `Q: can't put down in a base` and Q says NOT IN A BASE. A napkin's preview is described [below](#draping-a-napkin). The preview is drawn in the first-person pass, over the world and over the object in the hands, which stands just above the spot and would otherwise hide it. It shows only while this player's living hotdog carries something in a round (not dead, not in the demo, not at the card table, not between rounds), and it stays when H hides the hints: it is the put's own feedback, not a tip. It asks the simulation once a frame and reuses the same few entities, moving them and swapping their materials. Online it asks the client's own copy of the round, about its own hotdog as the client predicts it, holding what the server's frames say it holds.
- **The trigger throws it** along the aim, lobbed 0.5 m/s upwards: at 1.6 m/s under 0.2 kg, 1.1 m/s up to 0.6 kg, and slower the heavier beyond that (1.1 m/s times the square root of 0.6 kg over the load: a cereal box 1.06 m/s, a cutting board 0.57 m/s), so the heavier goes the shorter way (`feast::throw_speed`). That is the way to send something far. Behind the forcefield the trigger still throws (only the guns are locked).
- **Nothing may be put down in a Forts base**, where the bacon is scored (each team's `base` zone): a put or a drape whose outline would reach into either team's base is refused, and the object stays in the hands. Whatever is thrown, knocked, shoved or let go into a base is moved out of it once it comes to rest: slid out by the base's nearest side that keeps it on the counter, half a centimetre clear, to settle there (`Feast::clear_bases`).
- Neither a put nor a carried object goes through a block or the forcefield, and a hotdog that dies lets go.

What a fortress is worth: every solid loose object stops rounds, so a cutting board stood on its edge, the bread bin, a napkin, a plate, a mug, a tin or a shaker (and a chair, a crate, the bowl or the tray) is cover; a cereal box, a roll of paper towels or a pillow only slows a round and weakens it (see [materials and rounds](#materials-and-rounds)), so a wall of cereal boxes is a soft one. Against bodies a locked piece is a wall; one that is loose is a matter of weight, by the loose objects' ordinary physics: a hotdog shoves one pillow, chair or crate along almost without slowing, two crates stacked cost it a third of its pace, and three crates in a pile or a row stop it, so that it has to go round. The physics has the limits its source states (`crates/kitchen-sim/src/props.rs`): edges are only tested at sample points, so two objects can sink a centimetre or two into each other before a corner catches. Walking onto a loose object lower than a step (a napkin, a cutting board lying flat, a pillow), a hotdog steps up onto it and stays on it as it walks (it used to be let down to the counter at every stride and climb back on, which held it to under half its pace and shoved the object ahead, so that next to a chair or a crate it could get stuck).

### Draping a napkin

A napkin is a big cloth one, a tea towel's size: 45 cm square and 90 g, light enough to carry at full pace and to throw as far as anything. It is solid (folded cloth stops a round), lying flat or draped. Laid on the bare counter it lies there, in soft folds. **Put or thrown, it drapes over whatever it comes to rest on** (`drape.rs`, `Feast::canopy`):
  - over other loose objects (its middle at least 3 cm above the counter): it and the loose objects whose middles lie under it (and which do not stand out above it) are frozen where they are, and in their place stands a wall: a block over the outline of what it covers (1 cm wider all round), up to the napkin's top;
  - over the edge of something raised that it lies on, a block, a deck, a ramp, an earlier drape's wall, or the counter itself, with at least 6 cm of it past the edge (18 cm past the counter's): it is frozen where it lies, its cloth hangs down over the edge, and its hanging part is a strip of wall 1 cm thick against that side, up to the napkin's top, which covers what is behind it (on the counter's edge the strip is the counter's last centimetre);
  - over a mixture (a block's edge and a crate beside it): one wall over all of it, the gap between included.

  On screen the cloth is laid over the tops of what it covers (loose objects and blocks), sagging between them, with its sides hanging in folds down to the counter; over an edge alone it is the napkin's own cloth, lying where it froze and hanging over the edge. Laid flat on the bare counter or a deck, or with only a few centimetres over an edge, it simply lies there.

  **While a napkin is carried, the [placement preview](#carrying) shows what Q would do with it.** Where it would drape into a wall, its ghost is **green**, the wall it would make is outlined in green (its top at the napkin's top, its corners down to the counter, a faint pane between them), each loose object it would cover glows green, and the tooltip says `Q: drape over 4 items` (or `over the edge`). Where it would only lie flat its ghost and its outline are **grey** and the tooltip says `Q: put down flat (no pile to drape)`. A drape that the landing would refuse (below) is shown grey too, and the tooltip says why: the preview asks the simulation's own refusals (`Feast::refusal`, which `tent` drapes by), so it never shows a wall that is not built. In Forts that check builds a navigation grid (0.13 to 0.32 ms natively on the bundled maps), so the client asks it again only when the drape it previews, the walls or the bodies near it change.

- Nobody walks through the wall or shoves it: hotdogs and cats are stopped 3 cm from it as at any block, and routes (bots', cats') go round it.
- Rounds stop at its face (whatever it covers: a draped wall is solid, pillows and all), a glob's splash does not reach past it, and a blast does not move it or anything in it.
- Its top is a floor: a hotdog that gets up there (a stim jump clears a napkin over one crate) stands on it.
- A body standing where the wall appears is moved out to its nearest side.
- A napkin does not drape (it stays a loose napkin on top, and tries again once it has been moved and come to rest) where, in Forts, its wall would reach into a team's base, where its wall would cover the bacon's spot or a zombie spawn point, where a body in its way cannot be moved out, or, in Forts, where it would leave either team's base with no way on foot to the bacon: **a sealed base cannot make the bacon uncapturable**.
- **Pulling it off**: Q aimed at the wall (or standing against it) pulls the napkin into the hotdog's hands, and what was under it is loose again where it lay. **Anyone may, at any time, in every mode**: in Forts during the build (behind the forcefield, so only its own side can reach it) and during the fight as well, by either team, so a fortress is broken into by pulling its napkins off. Bots pull a wall only when walls have cut them off in a Forts fight (see the Forts bots above). Draping is allowed throughout too, in the fight as in the build.

The simulation keeps the drapes in `drapes` ({id, napkin, props, rect, top}, in snapshots too; a snapshot from before calls `napkin` `napkin`, which is read as well), and a draped prop's state is 5.

### The electric griddle

Every Forts map has an electric countertop griddle across the middle of the counter, a little in from its front edge and on the centre line, so that it is as near one team as the other: a dark non-stick plate 50 cm by 28 on short legs, 6 cm high (more than a step, so a hotdog jumps onto it, and a bot never does), with a grease channel and a drip tray along its front, two cool handles, a cord and plug out of its back, and the thermostat dial and an indicator light in the middle of its front, which faces the middle of the counter. It is a block of the style `griddle` (`facing` is the side with the dial; `crates/kitchen-sim/src/griddle.rs` holds its rules and `src/griddle.rs` draws it; the editor places it among the kitchen things, and a map that has one is version 2), and a map may have any number of them. It starts off.

- **Switching.** A hotdog within 25 cm of the dial and facing it presses E (use) and the griddle goes on or off: anyone, of either team, in any phase of a round, the forcefield or not. A griddle will not flip again for 0.3 s. The server decides (online the client flips its own copy at once and the server's word corrects it), and the hotdog that last switched it *on* is remembered. By the crosshair a key cap and a power symbol say that E switches it, while one is in reach and faced; the symbol is amber while it is on. A click sounds, for whoever flips it, quieter the further off.
- **Temperature**, in degrees Celsius, from 21 (room temperature). On, the plate heats towards 205 at 9 degrees a second, easing off over the last 24 (about 25 seconds from cold); off, it cools towards 21 at 3 a second, easing over the last 16 (a little over a minute from the top).
- **The readout** floats over each griddle in the world: a thermometer, the temperature in whole degrees with a drawn degree mark (the font has none) and a C, and a dot that is lit green while the griddle is on and grey while it is off, all on a dark pill, coloured by the heat from cool blue through white and warm yellow (100) and orange (150) to red (205). It faces the viewer, grows with nearness (the digits 18 to 36 pixels at a 720-pixel window: 24 at 1.2 m, still 18 from across the counter), fades behind a block or a loose object and a long way off (gone by 9 m), and is kept just under the head-up display's top line when the griddle is above the top of the screen.
- **Heat.** As the plate warms it glows, a dull red along its edges, and over 120 degrees it smokes, more the hotter it is; where a hotdog stands on it hot enough to burn, grease spits from under its feet and it sizzles (`assets/audio/sizzle.wav`, a loop that is louder the hotter the plate and the nearer the hotdog; `tools/build_griddle_sounds.py` makes it and the switch's click).
- **Burns.** A hotdog standing on the plate (feet on its top; not jumping over it) loses HP at a rate that is nothing at 100 degrees or less and rises in a straight line to 25 HP a second at 205, so 100 HP last four seconds on a plate at the top. It is damage of its own kind, BURN, that no stim doubles: the kill feed shows a flame, and the kill is credited to whoever last switched the griddle on (nobody, for a hotdog that burns on one it switched on itself). In Free For All and the sandbox that kill counts as any other (the god mode and the spawn shield protect from it as they do from the rest). Nobody burns while the forcefield stands (nobody can be hurt), at a card table or after the round has been decided.
- **Bots** walk off a plate that is on or warm (over 50 degrees) by its nearest clear side, and do nothing else until they are off it.
- **Online** (protocol 12 as it was: its frames are unchanged). The server sends `{type:'griddle',griddles:[{block,on,temp,by}]}` as JSON text, as the card table travels: when a client sits down, whenever a griddle is flipped or a round begins, and every second; a client carries each temperature on between by the same law, and a press of E at a dial is the client's `{type:'griddle'}`, which flips the griddle in reach of that socket's own hotdog, if there is one. A map without a griddle sends nothing.

### Forts maps and the editor

A map made for Forts is a map with `"mode": "forts"`; any map with the Forts layout (a base for each team, a field and the bacon's spot in its `zones`) can be played as Forts, and a map made for Forts as a siege or Free For All. Beside the usual lists:

- `"build"`: read and written back (5 to 900) but passed over: the build time is the game's rule (150 s, the server's `--build`, the page's `?build=`).
- each start with a `"team"`, `"mustard"` or `"ketchup"`: `{"p": [x, z], "yaw": a, "team": "mustard"}`. A team's hotdogs take its starts in order.
- `"zones"`, a list of three kinds of mark: `{"kind": "base", "team": "mustard", "rect": [minX, minZ, maxX, maxZ]}` for each team, `{"kind": "field", "rect": [...]}` for the forcefield (a thin area right across the map; it divides the map along its shorter side) and `{"kind": "bacon", "p": [x, z]}`.
- the stock is simply the loose objects among `props` (each side's are those on its side of the field), and the weapons are the ordinary `pickups` (guns and boxes of ammunition, which give and come back as in every gun mode: see [basics](#basics-every-gun-mode)).

The editor's zone tools (Team base, Forcefield, Bacon) work on every map: the zones are layout, and the problems list checks the Forts layout wherever it is whole (and says when it is only part of one). The Game panel's mode is the mode the map was made for, the one it plays when nothing else chooses; the editor has no build time or pickup counts or times to set, since those are the game's rules.

Every map now says its `mode` (`"siege"` unless it says otherwise) and has a `zones` list (empty for a siege). What a Forts map leaves out is filled in so that it still plays: a strip across the middle for the field, the end eighth of the map for a base, the middle of the field for the bacon, the side a start stands on for its team.

In the editor the Counter panel has the mode and the build time, and a Forts map gains three tools: Team base (H), Forcefield (W) and Bacon (N). A map has one base for each team, one field and one bacon, so drawing another moves the one there is. A base and a start have a Team field in the inspector; the plan shows bases in their team's colour, the field as a blue strip, the bacon on its plate and each start ringed in its team's colour, and the 3D view shows the field as blue glass. Switching a map to Forts puts out both bases, the field and the bacon and gives each start the team of the side it stands on. The problems list checks that a Forts map can be played: both bases there and on opposite sides of a field that closes the map (nobody can walk round it during the build), neither base lying across it, every start with a team and on its team's side, three starts a side, the bacon out of every block and within reach on foot of both teams once the field is down, each base within reach of the bacon (so that it can be carried home), and something to build with on each side. For any map it also warns when the counter is bigger than the largest kitchen the game builds (see [the kitchen](#the-kitchen)): one reaching past x = ±4.5 or beyond z = 5.

The bundled Forts map is Countertop Fort, [assets/maps/pillow-fort.json](assets/maps/pillow-fort.json) (the file keeps its old name, so that links to it go on working): six metres of counter by 1.8 (the siege's is 3.6 m by 1.36 m), symmetric, with the bacon on a low deck in the middle that a ramp climbs from each side, a raised deck at the back of each half, a full-size microwave (62 cm wide, 48 deep and 36 tall: on every Forts map the microwave is one at a hotdog's true scale) against the back of the middle, an [electric griddle](#the-electric-griddle) across the middle by the front edge and a base at each end; the kitchen grows to hold it, its side walls 0.7 m past the bases.

## Detailed original player models

The live game shares high-resolution curved casing, sculpted split buns, raised mustard, smooth facial details, and jointed limbs across five player colors. Every hotdog, in every mode, has the same friendly face: big bright eyes under softly raised brows, rosy cheeks and a smile, which opens while its player speaks; the mustard squiggle stops under the chin. The colour a player chooses in multiplayer's lobby is not on the face: its hotdog wears it on the ring round its waist, and its name tag is written in it. Its animation is procedural (`src/player_model.rs`): a run with knee lift and opposing arm swing (past a full run, 0.67 m/s, the legs turn over faster with the pace, so a stride covers as much ground at the 1.25 m/s walk and stimmed as at a run), mirrored left and right strafes with the arms out for balance, backpedalling, a crouch and a crouch-walk, a prone crawl, recoil, a melee strike for each weapon, the stim clap, and arms thrown open by the winner. Original 2048 × 2048 albedo, normal, and roughness maps supply the food materials.

The original animated glTF model is included at [assets/models/hotdog-player.glb](assets/models/hotdog-player.glb), with its editable procedural source in [tools/build_hotdog_player.py](tools/build_hotdog_player.py). Rebuild the model with `python3 tools/build_hotdog_player.py`. Rebuild its 2K food maps with `/usr/bin/python3 tools/build_food_materials.py --size 2048` (Pillow required). See [model notes](assets/models/README.md).

## Browser

Install the Rust `wasm32-unknown-unknown` target, the wasm-bindgen CLI at the version Cargo.lock pins, `wasm-opt` ([binaryen](https://github.com/WebAssembly/binaryen/releases), or `cargo install wasm-opt`) and Node, then:

```sh
# Both builds: WebGPU into pkg-webgpu/ (browsers with a working WebGPU adapter), WebGL 2 into pkg/ (every other browser)
tools/build-web.sh
# The server: the page, both builds and the assets (and multiplayer, and the map editor) on port 8137
cargo build --release --manifest-path crates/server/Cargo.toml
crates/server/target/release/hotdog-server
```

`tools/build-web.sh webgl2` (or `webgpu`) builds one. Each build is compiled with Cargo's `web` profile (the release profile as one whole: fat LTO and one codegen unit, so a long link; native builds keep `release`), for WebAssembly's 128-bit SIMD by default (`-C target-feature=+simd128`, with `--enable-simd` for wasm-opt; `SIMD=0 tools/build-web.sh` builds without it, best in a target directory of its own, since a change rebuilds everything; every current browser runs SIMD, Chrome and Edge 91, Firefox 89, Safari 16.4 and later, and the page tells one that cannot why the game does not start), bound with wasm-bindgen, optimised again with `wasm-opt -O3` and stripped of its function names, and given brotli and gzip copies of its module and script. The server sends a browser that takes them the compressed copy (about 7 MB instead of a 45 MB module; the build used to be 135 MB, sent whole), as long as the copy is no older than the file, so a build put in place another way is sent as it is. Every file has an ETag, so a returning browser keeps its copies after a 304 instead of downloading them again. The module with its names stays out of reach in `target-web/symbols/`, for reading a panic's stack: `wasm-opt target-web/symbols/pkg/hotdog_simulator_bg.wasm --print-function-map` names each `wasm-function[N]`. The food textures load as lossy WebP copies (quality 92) that the script writes beside the PNGs: 1.4 MB instead of 14 MB. `TARGET_DIR`, `OUT` and `LOCK` (a file to `flock` while `pkg/` is written) are read from the environment.

While it loads, the page shows how much of the build has arrived, then the files the game fetches as it starts. Any static file server still serves the game, uncompressed.

The page loads the WebGPU build when the browser offers a WebGPU adapter and the WebGL 2 build otherwise, and returns to WebGL 2 if the WebGPU build fails while starting (one reload, remembered for a week so that Auto does not try it again). The renderer menu on the frame-rate line chooses one and the page remembers it (see [the graphics menus](#the-graphics-menus)); `?webgl2` or `?webgpu` in the address forces one. A server must offer both folders at its root, `pkg/` and `pkg-webgpu/` (`tools/build-web.sh` with no argument writes both into `OUT`, the repository by default; a root of symlinks needs a link for each): with only `pkg/` a WebGPU-capable browser asks for `pkg-webgpu/`, finds nothing and quietly plays the WebGL 2 build. The live server's root (a folder of symlinks) needs both links, and WebGPU only reaches a page opened over https:// or from localhost (an SSH tunnel to localhost counts; plain http from another machine does not, and the page stays on WebGL 2).

Open <http://localhost:8137/?demo> for the automatic five-player demo or <http://localhost:8137/?play> to play (any static file server will do for these two, `python3 -m http.server` for one). WebGPU or WebGL 2 is required. Native play needs a graphical session and Bevy's Linux libraries (X11/Wayland, ALSA, and udev). How much is drawn suits the device (see [graphics](#graphics)); CPU renderers draw no shadow maps. The scope is generated in memory and has no external UI image dependency.

### Graphics

The game picks graphics that suit the device it runs on and keeps adjusting them while it plays, so that it stays smooth on a laptop's integrated graphics and keeps the full look on a strong GPU (`src/quality.rs`). The frame-rate line in the bottom right ends with the build running and what the game chose, the level and the render scale: `60 FPS · WebGL 2 · Auto · Low · 70%`. Each of the last three opens a menu to choose it by hand (see [the graphics menus](#the-graphics-menus)).

**WebGPU against WebGL 2, measured** (Chrome 157, headless, an Intel Arc B580 over Vulkan, 1280×720, a fixed camera in FFA, Pillow Fort and the Kung Fu dojo, three runs each, alternating): at the same feature set (Lowest, or High with `?quality=lite`) the two builds run within about ±10% of each other, 4 ms a frame at Lowest and 6 to 9 ms at High, and the page's main thread is busy about 100% of the time in both, so it is the game's own WebAssembly that sets the pace here, not the GPU API; WebGPU's High draws more (four shadow cascades, contact shadows, volumetric light, ambient occlusion, TAA) at the same speed. Its module is 0.7% larger (48.9 MB against 48.6 MB, 7.8 MB with brotli either way) and its first frame at High about a second later. A CPU renderer (SwiftShader) cannot run it: the device is lost a few seconds in, and the page then reloads on WebGL 2. Not measured: a machine whose WebGL 2 goes through a slow translation layer, where WebGPU may do better.

**Levels.** High is the look each build was made for: everything in the WebGPU build and natively; in the WebGL 2 build its own set (one shadow cascade, MSAA, HDR with bloom, distance fog, reflections, film grain). The others give up first what costs most and shows least:

**Render scale.** In a browser the canvas can be drawn with fewer pixels than the screen has: the page draws the canvas element at `--render-scale` of its size and magnifies it back to fill the window, so the browser hands the game a smaller drawing buffer, and the game overrides the window's scale factor so that the head-up display keeps its size and the pointer its place. Each level has a range of scales; a level starts at the top of its range (on a 2x screen High draws every device pixel, as the game always did) unless the device's budget says fewer, though never under 0.7 pixels per CSS pixel. Changing the scale is seamless. A picture drawn under the screen's resolution at Medium, Low or Lowest is sharpened before the page stretches it (`src/visuals/sharpen.rs`: each pixel pushed away from the average of its four neighbours but never past the lightest and darkest of them, the more the further the picture is stretched, 0.2 at 90% to 0.9 at 55% and under; one pass of five taps over the drawn pixels, after FXAA and before the head-up display; Bevy's own contrast-adaptive sharpening cannot run in WebGL 2), and its textures are sampled as finely as the screen's own resolution would (a mip bias of the scale's base-2 logarithm), so that it does not blur into the stretch (`?sharpen=0` turns the sharpening off, `?sharpen=0.5` sets its strength); the generated surfaces (the counter, the walls, the floor, the wood) are filtered anisotropically (8×) at every level, in WebGL 2 too. Natively the scale is always 1.

**The start.** Before the game starts the page reads the GPU's name (WebGL's unmasked renderer, and the WebGPU adapter's vendor, architecture and description), `navigator.deviceMemory`, the number of processor threads, the screen and whether this is a phone or a tablet; the game maps them to a level and a budget of pixels:

| Device | Starts at | Pixels drawn at most |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discrete GPU (GeForce GTX and RTX, Radeon RX, Arc A and B series) | High | all |
| Apple silicon | High | 3.7 million |
| Newer integrated GPU (Arc graphics of Core Ultra, Radeon 680M to 890M, Snapdragon X), GeForce MX and the like | Medium | 2.1 million |
| Integrated GPU (Intel HD, UHD, Iris, Iris Xe; AMD Vega and Radeon Graphics) | Low | 1.2 million (70% of a 1080p screen) |
| A GPU that is not recognised | Medium, or Low with 4 GB or less or 4 threads or fewer | 2.1 (1.2) million |
| Phone or tablet | Low | 1 million |
| CPU renderer (SwiftShader, llvmpipe) | Lowest | 0.6 million |

**Auto.** With Auto chosen the game watches its frame time and, in a browser, how much of each frame its main thread spends working (the rest of a frame held at the display's rate is spare), ignoring the first ten seconds, single long frames (a shader compiling, a round starting) and the gap while the tab was hidden. It judges a robust pace over the last two and a half seconds: a game that reads 56 to 64 frames a second around a 60 Hz display holds 60. When it stays under 50 frames a second for three and a half seconds it steps down, features before sharpness: on a 1x screen High at 100% and 90%, Medium at 90%, Low at 90% and 80%, then Lowest at 80% and 70%. That is the floor: 0.7 pixels per CSS pixel with every feature off. Only a game still under 24 frames a second there goes under it, as a last resort, to 62%, 55% and 50% (half the page's resolution), one step after every 8 seconds that slow, and climbs back over the floor as soon as it runs at 32 frames a second or better. Fewer pixels are given up only where they buy something: a step of the resolution is skipped when the main thread is at work for most of a slow frame (the processor is what holds it back), and one that does not make the frame at least a tenth shorter is taken back at once; either way, for the rest of the visit the resolution stays where it is and climbs back to 100% (the level's finest), and only the levels move, and only those that cost the processor less than the one above (`quality::CPU`). It climbs back too, sharpness before features: from wherever it is, the next finer scale and then the next better level. Held at the display's rate with room on the main thread, it tries the next step after 4 seconds (a better level after 6, and only when the main thread's measured work, grown by what that level costs it, still fits the frame); without that measure (natively) after 8 seconds held at the display's rate or well above 50, a better level after 15 seconds above 70 frames a second or 40 held at 60 Hz. A step up has to hold the display's rate on its first full look or it is taken back; that rung is then not tried again for 30 seconds, twice as long after each further failure (up to half an hour), so a level the machine cannot hold costs a short hitch now and then, not a stutter. Where it has held smoothly for 15 seconds is remembered per device (in local storage), and the next visit starts there, as a start only: the climb tries upward from it as soon as there is room (an older version's setting under the floor, such as Lowest at 50%, starts on the floor).

**In short:** Auto keeps the frame rate over 50 by turning features off first and the resolution down only to 70%, and only where fewer pixels make the frame shorter; on a machine held back by its processor it leaves the resolution at 100% and moves the levels. With the frame held at the display's rate and room to spare on the main thread it climbs back to the best level that holds it, quickly, and backs off from a level that does not hold for longer each time. It trades sharpness for speed beyond 70% only for a game that would otherwise run under 24 frames a second. To pin the resolution, choose it in the resolution menu on the frame-rate line (or the game's settings): 100% to 50% holds whatever the level, and Auto quality then moves between the levels only; `?scale=80` in the address does the same and wins over the menu, and `?quality=lowest` (or any level) pins the level.

With `?check` in the address, `window.hotdogDetail = {scale: 0.7, msaa: false, ...}` overrides any part of the level's detail (`null` lets go), and `window.hotdogBurn = ms` makes every frame take that many more milliseconds for each million pixels drawn (less at lower levels), as a slower GPU would, to try the governor.

What each part costs, measured in WebGL 2 on an Arc B580 at 3840×2160 (8.3 million pixels, where that GPU is the limit), as the change in frame time from High in the kitchen, Pillow Fort and the jungle: drawing 70% of the pixels each way −34 to −45%; the minimal set −28 to −43%; the theme's lamps all off −10 to −25%; MSAA off −19 to −21% (with FXAA instead −11 to −16%); HDR and bloom off −10 to −14%; fog off −6 to −9%; reflections off 0 to −6%; the jungle's plants at their fewest −13%; the sun's shadows off −5%; a third of the rays −2% (−7% in the jungle); the shadow map at 2048 or 1024, the kitchen lamps' shadows and the film grain within ±5%. At 1920×1080 that GPU is not the limit (the game's own work, about 5 ms a frame on a Ryzen 9 5950X, is): there the kitchen lamps' shadows cost 12% of Pillow Fort's frame, and Low and Lowest still save 15–18% and 31–38% of it.

#### The graphics menus

The frame-rate line's renderer, quality and resolution (`WebGL 2 · Auto · Low · 70%`) each open a small menu, marked with the choice in force; the page remembers each choice in local storage, and the address wins over it and locks its menu, which then says why. They work with the mouse, by touch (each part's tap target reaches past its small text to 32 px and more, and the menus fit a phone) and from the keyboard (Tab to a part; Enter, Space or the arrows open it; the arrows, Home and End move; Enter or Space chooses; Left and Right go to the next menu; Esc closes). The line takes clicks in the multiplayer lobby, in the fridge and before a round; during a round online the clicks are the game's and the line lets them through, and F8 still steps the quality. Offline, where there is no lobby and the round starts as the page loads, it takes them wherever the links to the other modes do, so not while the touch controls are out (their ☰ menu brings it back). The page's side is in `lobby.js` (`graphics`), which every page loads for it, and is tested by `tools/graphics.test.mjs`.

- **Renderer: Auto, WebGPU, WebGL 2.** Each is a build of its own, so a change reloads the page (online, back into the lobby), keeping the rest of the address. Auto loads the WebGPU build where the browser has a working WebGPU adapter and WebGL 2 elsewhere. If the WebGPU build then dies while starting (or a build it cannot compile or start is served), the page reloads once on WebGL 2 and writes the time to local storage (`hotdog-webgpu-failed`): for a week Auto loads WebGL 2 without trying, the menu says why, and choosing WebGPU or Auto there forgets it and tries again. A WebGPU the player chose is tried on every visit, and when it fails the reload carries `?webgl2`, which cannot loop. WebGPU is shown disabled, with the reason, where the page cannot have it: in a browser without it, or on a page opened over plain http from another machine (browsers offer WebGPU only to https:// and localhost). `?webgl2` or `?webgpu` wins.
- **Quality: Auto, High, Medium, Low, Lowest**, applied at once; F8 steps through them too (natively as well). A level picked by hand is drawn at the top of its range within a cap on pixels (Medium 3.7 million, Low 2.1, Lowest 1.2) and is not adjusted. `?quality=auto|high|medium|low|lowest` wins, as do the old names: `full` is High, `lite` is High with the WebGL 2 set, `minimal` is Lowest. Natively it is `--quality`. A profiling run (`--profile`, `?profile`) holds its level.
- **Resolution: Auto, or 100%, 90%, 80%, 70%, 60% or 50%**, applied at once: the share of the screen's pixels drawn along each side. Auto is the render scale above (the governor's, or a hand-picked level's own). A fixed share holds whatever the level, past the level's range and its cap if need be; under Auto quality the governor then moves between levels only, at that scale, and where it settles is not remembered (it is not where Auto would settle). Back at Auto, the governor returns to the scale it had (the nearest the level has, if the level has moved meanwhile). `?scale=80` (or `80%`, `0.8`; 25% to 100%) wins. Natively there is no scale to fix.

### Ultra tier (native)

A top tier for a native build on a strong GPU, built on Bevy 0.19's two experimental renderers: **raytraced lighting** (`bevy_solari`) and **virtual geometry** (meshlets). It is compiled only with the `ultra` cargo feature and only natively; no browser build has it, and nothing changes unless a flag asks for it (`src/ultra.rs`, `src/ultra/`).

```sh
cargo run --release --features ultra -- --raytraced       # raytraced lighting
cargo run --release --features ultra -- --meshlets        # virtual geometry
cargo run --release --features ultra -- --ultra           # both asked for (also --quality ultra): see "Not together"
```

`--ultra` and `--quality ultra` hold the High level underneath. Without the feature the flags say how to build it and the game plays at High; in a browser `?raytraced` says the same.

**What it needs.** Native Vulkan (or DX12 for the lighting, Metal for the geometry). For the lighting: hardware ray queries (`VK_KHR_ray_query` with acceleration structures) and descriptor indexing, as on NVIDIA RTX, AMD RX 6000 and later, and Intel Arc. For the geometry: 64-bit image atomics (`shaderImageInt64Atomics`), subgroups and depth clip control, as on recent NVIDIA, AMD and Intel GPUs. The game asks the GPU before it starts and leaves off, with a message, a half the GPU cannot run. Developed and measured on an Intel Arc B580 with Mesa 26.2.6.

**Raytraced lighting (`--raytraced`).** Solari lights what the camera sees by tracing rays through a second, traced copy of the scene (`src/ultra/gi.rs`):

- The sun on the ceiling lights the room as before, but its shadows are traced and soft. Its light bounces: the counter and the walls light each other and colour what is near them.
- Glowing things light what is around them: the kitchen's lamps and the dojo's lanterns (each point or spot light becomes a small glowing sphere in the traced scene, as bright as its lumens), bursts of relish, the griddle's hot ring and unlit glows like the sun's disc.
- Every shown, opaque, shadow-casting mesh of the world gets a traced stand-in that follows it each frame. A mesh Solari cannot take as it is (it wants position, normal, uv and tangent, with 32-bit indices) gets a traced copy, made again when the mesh changes. Moving hotdogs and items stay right.
- The game's opaque `StandardMaterial`s are drawn deferred (Solari lights the G-buffer). Custom materials (the scope's lens, faces) stay forward and are lit as before, and the gun is lit the ordinary way.

What it does not do, in Bevy 0.19.1:

- Solari knows only the sun and glowing surfaces. Point and spot lights are emulated as above, and ambient light, the generated room reflection and the sky are ignored. The game's camera is therefore exposed 1.5 stops brighter, since the traced room has no fill light.
- The ceiling is left out of the traced scene, so that the sun on it still reaches the counter (rasterised, it casts no shadow either). Rays going up through it, or out of the open front of the room, find nothing: polished metal facing them reflects black, and the ceiling's own bounce is missing.
- There is no denoiser except DLSS Ray Reconstruction, which is NVIDIA only. Solari's own temporal and spatial reuse plus the game's TAA average the noise, but the picture stays grainy, more so for a few frames after the view moves.
- Skinned and morphing meshes are not traced. (The game has none: the breakfast intro's people are rigid pieces moved every frame, and are traced like everything else.)
- Cost on the B580 (GPU time, the three maps measured): Solari's passes take 4.1 to 4.7 ms at 1280×720 and 8 to 10 ms at 1920×1080. The whole frame takes 5.1 to 6.0 ms and 9.8 to 11.5 ms, against 2.1 to 2.7 ms and 4.0 to 5.6 ms at High without it.

**Virtual geometry (`--meshlets`).** The meshlet renderer draws a few things far denser than the normal triangle budget, as real geometry with no normal map standing in for it (`src/ultra/geometry.rs`, `src/ultra/shapes.rs`):

- the counter top's honed stone, pitted and grained a tenth of a millimetre deep, with a 6 mm bullnose edge, on a 2 mm grid (2.6 million triangles for the bundled counter);
- a tiled backsplash on the back wall: glazed tiles 3 mm proud of their grout with rounded edges, 80,000 triangles each, over 200 in the kitchen and about 350 in Pillow Fort;
- the kung fu dojo's steamers, their woven lids as modelled strips (4 million triangles for the ring);
- cereal boxes (0.86 million triangles each) and plates (1.9 million each).

Each shape is made and converted on a background thread the first time, while the game's own mesh is still drawn, then cached in `~/.cache/hotdog-simulator/ultra` (or under `$XDG_CACHE_HOME`). The first run of a map takes from seconds to about two minutes before its dense shapes appear; later runs load them in a moment. Pillow Fort draws about 56 million source triangles this way, the kitchen 19 million, the dojo 27 million, against about 0.3 million rasterised. Meshlet LOD picks clusters to suit the view, so there is no level of detail to switch and nothing pops.

What it does not do, in Bevy 0.19.1:

- A camera drawing meshlets may have no prepass (Bevy's meshlet prepass binds the material at the wrong index) and no multisampling. With meshlets the game gives up TAA (FXAA instead), ambient occlusion and contact shadows.
- Each shadow view (four sun cascades and the ceiling spot) costs about 2 to 3 ms with meshlets in the scene, whatever is dense. That is most of the tier's cost: about 13 to 14 ms of GPU time at 1280×720 and 15 to 16 ms at 1920×1080 in all three maps, against 2 to 3 ms and 4 to 6 ms at High without it.
- Known problems: the counter's printed stone texture does not show on the dense counter, which is also darker than rasterised. In the Forts maps the dense cereal boxes and counter top are drawn unshaded, in the flat colour of the room's haze; the cause has not been found (they shade in the kitchen and the dojo).
- Only rigid things: a meshlet mesh moves, turns and scales as a whole (a knocked-over cereal box tumbles), but cannot be skinned, morphed or rebuilt each frame. Only opaque materials, and no normal maps baked from geometry.

**Not together.** In Bevy 0.19.1 (and 0.20.1) the two cannot run at once. Raytraced lighting needs a deferred prepass, and meshlets fail on any camera with a prepass. With that bug fixed in a copy of Bevy, the meshlets still come out black under the traced light, and an entity carrying both a meshlet mesh and a traced mesh hangs. So `--ultra` runs the raytraced lighting and says why.

## Multiplayer

A native game (and the Quest app) joins a server with `--connect HOST:PORT` (`ws://HOST:PORT/ws`), with a `ws://` or `wss://` address, or from the Esc menu: PLAY ONLINE keeps the choice in the settings, quits, and the next start joins the public server (`wss://hotdog-simulator.chilos.dev/ws`; `HOTDOG_SERVER` names another); PLAY SINGLE PLAYER undoes it. A `wss://` server is TLS (rustls, with Mozilla's roots compiled in).

One program serves everything, on one port: the page, both browser builds and the assets, the map editor, the game's WebSocket, each page's lobby WebSocket and the status. It is written in Rust on the game's own simulation (`crates/server`, binary `hotdog-server`; see [the server](#the-server)):

```sh
cargo build --release --manifest-path crates/server/Cargo.toml
crates/server/target/release/hotdog-server                    # this machine only: http://localhost:8137/?mp
crates/server/target/release/hotdog-server --host 0.0.0.0     # the network: http://HOST:8137/?mp
```

Players open `http://HOST:8137/?mp`, where HOST is the machine's LAN or VPN address; the page connects to the game at `ws://HOST:8137/ws` and to its lobby at `ws://HOST:8137/lobby` by itself (`wss://` when the page is https). Natively:

```sh
./target-native/x86_64-unknown-linux-gnu/release/hotdog-simulator --skip-intro --connect HOST:8137
```

The native client speaks the same WebSocket (`--connect ws://HOST:PORT/ws` names it in full). `--port N` moves the server (0 picks a free port) and `--root DIR` serves another copy of the repository (by default the working directory when it holds the game, or else the one the server was built in).

**One server plays every map and every mode**: the lobby's leader chooses the mode (siege, Forts, Free For All, Fully Loaded, Kung Fu or Titan) and the map of the next round, apart, or Random (see [the lobby](#the-lobby)). What the lobby starts on can be given: `--map <file>` (a map from the editor; one that is not in `assets/maps` can be chosen again under its file's name), `--map <name>` (`assets/maps/<name>.json`, for example `--map horde-night`), `--map random`, and `--mode siege|forts|ffa|cards|kungfu|titan`: the mode, played on `--map` (a siege on a Forts map: `--mode siege --map pillow-fort`), or without one on that mode's default map (`counter-siege`, `pillow-fort`, `kung-fu-dojo` or `titans-kitchen`), or with `--map random` the mode Random draws from. Without `--mode` a map plays the mode it was made for, and the lobby starts on `counter-siege`. A `--map` that cannot be played in the `--mode` (Forts without the Forts layout, a gun mode on the dojo, Titan anywhere but a map made for it, Fully Loaded anywhere but Counter Siege), or one that is not valid, stops the server with the reason. Its status is JSON at `http://HOST:8137/status`: what is being played (`map`, `mode`, `stage`), what is next (`choice`: `name`, `title`, `mode`, `theme`), who leads (`leader`), how many maps there are to choose from (`maps`), the players and the `protocol`.

### The server

`crates/server` is a Cargo package of its own, with its own lock file and target directory (`crates/server/target`), so building it never touches the game's dependency tree or rebuilds Bevy. It runs `kitchen_sim::feast::Feast`, the simulation single player and the client's prediction run, and adds what only a server needs: the lobby's stages, the seats (people taking over bots and bots taking over the seats people leave), each player's queue of commands, the names and colours, and the frames each client is sent. Its parts: `transport` (HTTP and WebSocket on axum: the router, each socket's task and its limits), `statics` (the files), `hub` (the game loop, one task that owns the lobby, the game and every socket's place in them: it steps the game sixty times a second by a monotonic clock and sends each game socket its own frame thirty times a second; see [the netcode](#the-netcode)), `lobby`, `game` and `protocol` (the messages). A socket may send messages of at most 16 KiB (text must be JSON; binary is a game socket's commands, and a lobby socket may send none), at most 180 a second, its first within five seconds, and falls more than 1 MiB behind at its peril; any of these ends it, as the Node server's limits did.

`--tick HZ` and `--send HZ` change the two rates (60 and 30), `--json` sends every game socket its frames as JSON (the debug encoding; a socket can also ask for it in its join), `--lag MS --jitter MS` hold every socket's messages back (half the round trip each way, give or take half the jitter, in order) to play over a slow network on purpose, `--ping S` sets how often every socket is sent a WebSocket ping (20 seconds; 0 sends none), which keeps a reverse proxy's idle timeout from cutting a lobby that is only sitting there (browsers and WebSocket libraries answer by themselves; a socket that has answered none of three pings in a row and sent nothing is let go as a dead connection), and `--stats S` prints every S seconds what the loop costs (milliseconds a step and a round of frames, steps it fell behind), what each game socket is sent a second, the size of a frame, how many were whole, and the server's resident memory.

It replaced a Node server: `tools/network/server.mjs` with its own simulation, lobby, Forts, bodies and loose objects (`simulation.mjs`, `lobby.mjs`, `forts.mjs`, `actor.mjs`, `props.mjs`), the page server `editor/serve.mjs`, their tests and the records that kept the two simulations in step (`tools/network/parity.json`, `fixtures/actor-golden.json`) are gone. Native clients now use the WebSocket too (they used plain TCP on port 9002), and the page connects to the server that served it (it used port 9001).

**As a service.** [tools/hotdog-server.service](tools/hotdog-server.service) runs the server as a systemd user service on port 8137 of every network the machine is on, restarted if it fails, its output in the journal. It assumes the repository is at `~/projects/hotdog-simulator` (edit its `WorkingDirectory` and `ExecStart` otherwise):

```sh
cargo build --release --manifest-path crates/server/Cargo.toml
mkdir -p ~/.config/systemd/user && cp tools/hotdog-server.service ~/.config/systemd/user/
systemctl --user daemon-reload && systemctl --user enable --now hotdog-server
journalctl --user -u hotdog-server -f          # its log
sudo loginctl enable-linger "$USER"             # keep it running while nobody is logged in
```

After a new build, `systemctl --user restart hotdog-server`.

### The lobby

Multiplayer starts in the lobby: while the players gather, every one of them is a hotdog shut in the fridge. The package lies at the back of the shelf with five franks asleep in its tray, one for each of the lobby's five seats, left to right (the sixth frank of the breakfast scene, the self-aware one, is not shown). As a player takes a seat, its frank wakes and gets up out of the tray and over to its place on the shelf in front of the package, where it stands under its name with the ring round its waist in the colour its player chose (in Forts, its legs and the ring and the back of its name in the colour of the side it would start on), turning to whoever is talking, mouth moving while that player speaks; when the player leaves, the frank lies back down in the tray. The door is shut, the lamp is dim and a strip of kitchen light shows at the door seal. Each player looks out from its own place (alone, at the franks still asleep) and looks around by moving the mouse. Up to five people fit, in either mode; a sixth is told the kitchen is full. When Forts is chosen the panel also has the team choice (Mustard Eaters, Ketchup Connoisseurs or Either) and the ready queue grouped by team, with the bots that make each side up to three; a siege has neither.

- **The leader and the next game.** The first player seated with a page leads the lobby, and keeps the lead until they leave; then it passes to the next in the order they arrived (a client without a page, the native one, has nowhere to choose from and never leads). A crown marks the leader in the ready queue, over their hotdog's name in the fridge and in the header. The leader chooses the mode (Siege, Forts, Free For All, Fully Loaded, Kung Fu or Titan; with Titan also who is the giant, see [Titan](#titan)) and the map, apart, from the maps in `assets/maps` (the server reads the folder, and lists each map by the name and look written in it, the mode it was made for, the modes it can be played in and why not the others), or Random, listed first: a map that can be played in the mode drawn when the fridge opens, never the one played last while the mode has another, and drawn again every time while Random stays chosen. Picking a mode keeps the map chosen where it can be played so, and otherwise goes to the map last chosen for the mode (at first its default, `counter-siege`, `pillow-fort` or the dojo); the page greys the maps the mode cannot be played on, with the reason. Picking a map keeps the mode the leader chose (a map that cannot be played in it is refused, with the reason); before a mode has been chosen (or while Fully Loaded or Kung Fu is), a map plays the mode it was made for. The choice can only be changed in the lobby, not during the countdown, the start or a round; everyone else sees it (with the controls greyed) and who made it, and a change makes everyone unready, since they agreed to something else. The server takes only the name of a map in the folder that it can play, a bare word: anything else is refused, with the reason, in a line to the leader. When the fridge opens on another map than the one being played, the server builds the round afresh for it: everyone keeps their seat, name, colour and the team they asked for, and every client is told the map and builds the arena from it (the kitchen grows or shrinks to fit). After the round, or the Forts match, everyone is back in the fridge and the leader can choose again.
- **The page.** In a browser the header reads "Waiting for game" over the number of players in the lobby (`02 / 05 · 1 ready`) and what is next (`Forts · Countertop Fort · kitchen`, or `Siege · Random map` until the fridge opens, then the map drawn), with the leader's name. A panel holds the next game (the mode, the map with its look, a line on the mode; the leader's controls), the player's name, the colour, the team in Forts, the ready queue (everyone in the order they arrived, with their colour and a ready mark), the Ready button and the microphone. It replaces the breakfast scene's narration card, which multiplayer never shows. In the round the head-up display names the map.
- **Name and colour.** A name is 1 to 16 visible characters, trimmed, with control and invisible characters removed; a name somebody else has (or a bot's) gets a number after it (`Frank 2`). The colour is one of red, blue, green, yellow and black: the hotdog wears it on the ring round its waist, and its name is written in it on the tag over its head (black, which would not show on a dark tag, in charcoal). The face is the same friendly face for everyone and takes no colour. The server keeps both, checks them, and sends them to everyone; they stay with the player from round to round, and the browser remembers them for next time. In the round the name tags over the hotdogs and the alerts use the chosen names, and each hotdog wears its colour. The colour is the player's own, not a team's: on a Forts map the team shows in the livery, its ring and its tag. Bots keep their built-in names and the colour of their place, as does everyone in single player.
- **Ready.** Each player presses Ready (again to take it back). When every player in the lobby is ready a three-second countdown runs; it stops if somebody takes their Ready back or a new player arrives. One player alone can ready up and play with four bots.
- **The start.** Then the same nine seconds play for everyone, timed by the server: the fridge door swings open and the kitchen's light floods in; the package is on the counter and its seal peels back; the players' hotdogs pop up in front of it; and the round's warm-up begins. (This is the breakfast scene's door and package without its narration, its pauses or Maya, who is not loaded in multiplayer: the carry from fridge to counter is a cut.)
- **After the round** the winner is shown for eight seconds and everyone returns to the fridge, not ready, until they ready up again.
- **Arriving during a round.** Somebody who connects while a round (or its start) is under way is given a slot for the next one and waits in the fridge, with the chat and the voice, while a bot goes on playing that slot; the page says "Round in progress". If everyone playing leaves, those waiting go straight to the lobby.
- **Text chat.** Enter opens the chat box (releasing the mouse if the game had it), Enter sends, Esc closes; while it is open the game hears no keys. Lines show the sender's name and colour, newest at the bottom; in the lobby they stay, during a round a line fades after a quarter of a minute and comes back while the box is open. Arrivals, departures, renames and "ready" appear as system lines. The server strips control characters, caps a line at 200 characters and each player at five lines every five seconds, and the page shows what arrives as text, never as markup.
- **Voice chat.** Browser to browser (WebRTC), one connection to each other player, with the server only passing the offers between them. The microphone is on when the page asks for it and is allowed; Mute silences it, and "Push to talk" keeps it silent except while V is held. A dot beside each name shows who has a microphone and lights while they speak (the same level, sent through the server a few times a second, moves the hotdog's mouth); the slider beside a name is that player's volume. **Browsers only give a page a microphone when it was opened over `https://` or from `localhost`**: opened over plain http from another machine the page says "Voice needs the page opened over https or from localhost" and the player can listen and type but not talk. An https page needs a `wss://` address for the server (the page derives `wss` by itself with `?mp`; the server speaks plain HTTP and WebSocket, so put a TLS proxy in front of its one port). There is no relay (TURN) server and by default no STUN server: two players hear each other only when their browsers can reach one another directly, as on one network or one VPN. Across the open internet, behind separate routers, voice will generally not connect (text chat and the game still work); `?stun=stun:HOST:PORT` names a STUN server, which helps with simple routers only.
- **Without a page.** The native client joins the lobby as a guest: `--name` and `--color` (or `--lips`, its old name) say what it is called and the colour it wears, a few lines of text stand in for the page (with what is next and who leads), and Enter says "ready". It has no chat and no voice, and cannot choose the game.

`hotdog-server --bots easy|normal|hard` sets how well the bots aim in every game (normal; see [bots](#bots)). `--countdown S` and `--opening S` set the two waits in seconds; in Forts `--build S` sets the build phase and `--stale S` the quiet seconds before the cats come. The native client asks for a team with `--team mustard` or `--team ketchup`, and R is its Ready in a build phase. With `--demo` there is no lobby: the bots play round after round, on the map the server started on, for whoever watches.

### The round

The server maintains five active hotdogs (six on a Forts map, three a side). Joining humans take over bot slots; bots take over disconnected players. The server owns the wave and its emergence (the ding, the ooze, the puddles and what is forming in them), HP, starvation, eating, ammunition, rounds in flight, melee strikes, the turrets, pests, crouch, prone, stim, friendly fire, the bodies and winner. A client sends what its player does, a numbered command a step, never where its hotdog stands: the server walks it there with the simulation's own rules, and the client predicts the same (see [the netcode](#the-netcode)). What the server sends is a frame: the snapshot below, as JSON clients knew it in protocol 11 and as the debug encoding still sends it, binary and only what changed otherwise. Snapshots carry each hotdog's velocity and whether it is on the ground, thrown or fallen; a client that does not find them assumes a hotdog standing still. They also carry whether it is crouching (`crouch`), the yaw each turret has turned to (`turrets`, in the map's order), and each hotdog's `last_damage`: what last hurt it (`kind`: 1 to 5 a round of that weapon, 6 a bite, 7 the turret, 8 a zombie, 9 pests, 10 hunger, 11 a fall off the counter, 12 a round of the syrup rifle, 13 a droplet of mayonnaise; 0 for nothing on record), the `attacker` if a hotdog did it, where it came from (`p`), the way it was travelling (`d`), when (`time`) and how much HP it took (`amount`). The dead are past hurting, so a dead hotdog's record is exactly what killed it, and the death animation reads it; a client that does not find these assumes a standing hotdog, turrets as the map has them, and works the cause of a death out from what it can see, as before. A client's commands say whether it is crouching and when it strikes (the melee strike, which is the bite in the free for all), and whether it aims down its sights (`scope` in the debug encoding; each hotdog's `aimed` says whether it does). The server owns the loose objects too: a snapshot carries `props`, the place, rotation and velocities of every loose object (a binary frame only those that changed since the frame it is encoded against, so a sleeping one is not sent again), and `knocks`, their hard impacts, which the client sounds, and `drapes`, the draped napkins ({id, napkin, props, rect, top}; a draped prop's `state` is 5), which the client stands as walls in its own copy of the level as the server does. Each of a snapshot's `pickups` is {id, p, weapon (0 a box of ammunition), ammo, ready (the round's time it is there again), respawn (the seconds it takes to come back once taken)}: a client draws its pad's ring from them. Each hotdog's `ammo` and `owned` have eight places (weapon 0, the hands full, and the seven guns). In a binary frame a hotdog's record keeps the rounds of weapons 0 to 6 where protocol 12 always had them, and the mayo squirter's come after its last damage, so a client of the six guns still reads a server of the seven and the other way round (a mask of what it holds has room for the eighth). A round that falls (the mayonnaise) is sent as it left the nozzle, with when, which does not change while it flies, and the client puts it on its arc: a stream costs a record a droplet and nothing more. The client keeps its own copy of the loose objects, moves it between snapshots and shoves it with its own hotdog, so a push answers at once; a copy that strays from the server's takes the server's place. Snapshots carry the lobby as well: the stage is `Lobby` or `Opening` between rounds, with `countdown` (seconds to the start, or null), `opening` and `opening_for` (seconds into the start sequence, and how long it lasts), and each hotdog's `name`, `color` (empty for a bot's or anyone's who has not chosen: its ring keeps the colour of its place), `talk` (how loudly its player is speaking, 0 to 1), `human`, `ready`, `waiting` and, for a slot somebody plays, that player's `profile`. A hotdog's `carry` is the loose object it holds (-1 for none; that object's `state` in `props` is 2 and its place is in front of the hotdog), and a client's commands say when to grab (pick up, or put down what is held) and to throw. In Forts a snapshot's stage is `Build`, `Fight` or `RoundOver` during the match, each hotdog has its `team` (0 the Mustard Eaters, 1 the Ketchup Connoisseurs) and whether it is `ready` for the field to drop, and `forts` holds the mode's state: `scores`, `rounds` (3), `match`, `build` and `ends` (when the build phase ends, on the round's clock), `field` (whether it is up), `bacon` (`state` 0 on its spot, 1 carried, 2 dropped; `holder`; `p`; `idle` seconds since it was dropped), `stale` (seconds to the next stalemate alarm), `alarms`, and after a round `winner` (a team, or -1), `reason` (`capture` or `wipe`) and `champion` (the team that has won the match). The lobby's roster names the `mode` (the next round's), the `leader` (a member id, or null), the next round's `choice` ({name, title, mode, theme}, or Random: {name: 'random', title: 'Random', mode, random: true}), the map being played (`playing`, the same shape; what Random drew once the fridge has opened) and the `maps` there are to choose from, and in Forts the teams, the score, each member's `team` (what it asked for) and `side` (where it is or would be), and the `bots` on each side; a page sends `{type:'team',team}`, and the leader's page `{type:'choose',mode}` or `{type:'choose',map}` (a map's file name, or `random`; with both, they must agree), which is refused with a `notice` saying why. Snapshots carry the lobby's `leader` (the hotdog the leader plays, 0 for none), its `choice`, and in Forts `sides` (the side each seated player's hotdog is on or would start on, by hotdog id), which the fridge shows. When the fridge opens on another map than the one being played, the server builds a new simulation for it and, before its first snapshot, sends every game socket `{type:'map',id,map}`: the map's canonical text and the hotdog this client now plays (ids may change); the client builds the arena again from it and takes the next snapshot as a new round's. Round numbers go on rising across the change. Clients and server must be built from the same revision: the join handshake is protocol version 12 (`{type:'join',version:12}` with the page's lobby `token`, or without one the `name`, `color` and `team` asked for, and `json: true` for the debug encoding; `{type:'ready',ready}` on the game socket is the native client's Ready, in the lobby and in a Forts build phase). The server plays the map the lobby chose (it starts on `counter-siege`, or what `--map` and `--mode` say: see [multiplayer](#multiplayer); an invalid one stops the server with the reason), and its welcome carries the map being played, so every client builds the same arena. Every client uses the server's WebSocket at `/ws`, and every page its lobby's at `/lobby`, on the port that serves the page: a browser by itself, natively `--connect HOST:8137`. The lobby's socket stays JSON. For LAN play, run the server with `--host 0.0.0.0` and connect using the host's LAN address. A page can be pointed at another server with `?mp&server=ws://HOST:PORT/ws` (its lobby is that address's `/lobby`). HTTPS hosting needs a `wss://` endpoint. Server `--demo` enables automatic play for connected spectators.

### The netcode

The server is the authority on everything; a client sends only what its player does, and predicts its own hotdog so that it answers at once (protocol 12: `crates/kitchen-sim/src/net.rs` holds the format both ends use, `crates/server/src/hub.rs` and `game.rs` the server's side, `src/network.rs` and `src/arena/online.rs` the client's).

- **Time.** The server steps the round 60 times a second, by a monotonic clock: every step is due at a moment of its own, and a loop that wakes late takes every step it owes at once (at most twelve; further behind than that it lets the time go and says so in its log), so a slow step never slows the game. Steps are numbered from the server's start, and every frame says which it was taken at. It sends each client a frame every second step, 30 a second. Both rates are in the welcome (`--tick`, `--send`). Sixty steps a second cost the server about what thirty do (measured with `--tick 30`), since the loose objects' physics runs at 120 steps a second either way (see [the measurements](#what-it-costs)), and a step of input is 17 ms rather than 33.
- **Commands.** The client samples its input once a step (16.7 ms of its own clock, however fast it draws) and numbers each sample: the stick or WASD as a vector in the hotdog's own frame (to 1/127), the yaw (to a 65,536th of a turn) and pitch it looks along, the stances held (crouch, prone), whether the trigger is held, the gun in hand, and what was pressed since the last command (jump, stim, the melee strike, eat, pick up or put down, throw, a pickup by its number). Each frame of the game sends the new commands in a binary batch (`Batch`: about 10 bytes a command) with the two before them again, the newest frame it has decoded (its ack) and whether it asks for a whole frame. The server queues them per hotdog, passes over those it has had and any numbered more than 64 past the newest, keeps at most 32 (the oldest go first, what was pressed in them kept for the next), and applies one a step, two while more than two wait, but never more than one a step over time (it earns 1.02 a step and saves at most 8): a modified client can no more walk faster than it can claim a place. A command is applied as single player steers its hotdog (`Game::command`): the stances, the stim, `Feast::walk` at the pace the hotdog has (crouched 65%, prone 40%, stimmed 150%, carrying something heavy 80%, a quarter of it while thrown), the jump, the pitch, the gun; then the pickup, eating, the strike (the bite in the free for all), grabbing or putting down, the throw and the trigger, each refused by the simulation when it cannot be done. Each command takes exactly one step of the hotdog's body: the step of the round for the last applied, a step of its own for one before it, and in a step that applied none it is held still, for a tenth of a second at most (a player silent for longer falls and flies as everything else does). Ready (R in a Forts build) and the lobby's Ready stay JSON text messages.
- **Prediction.** Each command is applied at once to the client's own copy of its hotdog's body (`Feast::predict`: the same walk, jump and step, against the level, the other bodies where the server last had them and the client's own copy of the loose objects, which it shoves), and kept with the body as predicted after it. Every frame says the last command the server applied and where the body was after it (`Mine`: its feet, its vertical speed, what is left of a throw, whether it stands, is thrown, perches on a head or leans on a prop). When the prediction for that command agrees (within 1.5 mm, standing and thrown alike), nothing happens; otherwise the body is put where the server says and every command since is applied again, against the loose objects as they stand on this client without shoving them twice (`Feast::rehearse`). The difference is not shown at once: under 25 cm it is eased away (exponentially, at 12 a second: a tenth of it is left after 0.2 s), further it is taken at once. The body is drawn between its last two steps, so that it moves smoothly at any frame rate. Shots and strikes are shown and heard at once, as before; the server lands them, and the rounds come back in its frames.
- **The electric griddle** (text, see [the electric griddle](#the-electric-griddle)): `{type:'griddle',griddles}` from the server and `{type:'griddle'}` from a client, beside the frames, which are as they were.
- **Everything else** (the other hotdogs, the cats, the rounds in the air, splats and shots) is drawn a little in the past, between the two frames either side of a clock that runs two frame intervals and a step (about 83 ms) behind the newest frame. The clock keeps to the frames' own steps, following the earliest any frame of the last second arrived: it is put right at once when more than 0.15 s out, and otherwise runs up to a fifth fast or slow until it is where it should be. Past the newest frame a hotdog is carried on along its velocity for a tenth of a second at most, and a cat held where it was. The loose objects are the client's own copy, moved by the same physics between frames and put right by them, as before; the HUD, the round's stage and clock and this hotdog's HP and rounds come from the newest frame.
- **Frames.** What a frame says (the snapshot below) is first made into whole numbers: positions to the millimetre (loose objects and their rotations to 1e-5, draped walls to a tenth of a millimetre), angles to 1e-4 radians, speeds to a millimetre a second, times to the millisecond, HP to a hundredth, and countdowns as the moment they run out, so that they do not change from step to step. It is then encoded against the state of a frame that client acknowledged: for each table (the round's own numbers, the hotdogs, the cats, the emergences, the pickups, the rounds, the pests, the turrets, the loose objects, the drapes, the Forts sides) the records gone and those that changed, and for a changed record only the fields that changed and by how much, as LEB128 numbers; anything that did not change costs nothing. A frame also carries the checksum of the state it describes, the client's ack and body, and the shots, splats and knocks since the last frame it was sent, each sent once. The server makes the state once a frame and keeps each client's last 64 (two seconds); a client that has acknowledged nothing yet (it joined, or a new map has begun), whose ack is older than that, or that asks (a frame it could not decode, or whose checksum did not match) is sent a whole frame. A whole frame of the bundled siege is under 1 KB, of a 128-cat horde about 5 KB; a delta in a quiet siege is about 90 bytes.
- **The debug encoding.** A game socket whose join says `json: true` (or every one, with `--json`) is sent each frame whole as JSON text, `{type:'snapshot', tick, ack, mine, ...}` with every field below, and may send its commands as JSON: `{type:'input', ack, commands:[{seq, stick:[x, z], yaw, pitch, crouch, prone, fire, jump, stim, melee, eat, grab, throw, weapon, pickup}]}`. The checks' native-style peer (`tools/check_breakfast.mjs`) and the server's own socket tests use it; `tools/check_feast.mjs` reads `window.hotdogState` in the page, which `?check` fills as before, now with `net` (the prediction's corrections and snaps, how far they moved the hotdog, the commands not yet applied, how far behind the clock draws, the frames decoded, whole and broken), and `window.hotdogNet` counts the bytes the page's game socket has received and sent.
- **A slow network on purpose.** `hotdog-server --lag 150 --jitter 40` holds every message back, in and out, by half the round trip each way, give or take half the jitter, in order (as TCP delivers): for playing and testing the netcode over a slow link from this machine.

#### What it costs

Measured on a Ryzen 9 5950X with five load clients (Node scripts joining the game socket as guests that ready up, walk in circles at 60 commands a second, fire and jump now and then, and acknowledge every frame; the protocol-11 ones sent their claims 30 times a second), the server alone on a spare port, against the protocol-11 server it replaced (JSON, every snapshot whole, 30 a second). The server's CPU rose by a point or two of a core: twice as many messages come in (a batch every step), and each socket's frame is encoded for it.

| | Protocol 11, per client | Protocol 12, per client | Server CPU (one core) | Resident memory |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Siege (bundled map, 40 s from the warm-up) | 153 KB/s, peak 178 | 4.5 KB/s, peak 6.5 | 1.2% → 2.2% | 10.3 → 10.5 MB |
| Forts (Pillow Fort, the old stock of pillows: 30 s build, then the fight) | 206 KB/s, peak 315 | 8.8 KB/s, peak 21 | 2.0% → 3.9% | 12.2 → 12.5 MB |
| 128-cat horde (64 spawns, 40 s) | 456 KB/s (wave 593), peak 905 | 7.6 KB/s (wave 13.4), peak 34 | 1.5% → 2.5% | 10.7 → 11.6 MB |

A step costs 0.3 to 0.5 ms (Forts, five clients) and a round of frames for five clients under 0.2 ms (`--stats`). Five players each holding the trigger of a mayo squirter (60 droplets a second in the air between them, sweeping their aim) cost each client 6.0 KB/s, peak 8.3, where five hot sauce throwers cost 11.2 KB/s, peak 14.9: a droplet that falls is sent once, as it left the nozzle. At `--tick 30` a step costs about twice as much, and the CPU a second is the same.

## Free For All and the sandbox

**Free For All** is the third gun mode beside the siege and Forts (`crates/kitchen-sim/src/rules.rs`), with their [basics](#basics-every-gun-mode): the lobby's leader picks it, then any map but the dojo, siege or Forts (a Forts map's zones, forcefield and bacon are left out; its pickups are used), or Random from all of them. Everyone fights everyone, humans and bots, from the first second; there is no zombie wave, no hunger, no pests, and no bite or eating (F is the strike). Dying and coming back restores the loadout; a gun run dry still strikes. `mode=ffa ammo=inf` plays it with rounds that never run out, every magazine and reserve kept full, as it once was. Where the hotdogs appear is `crates/kitchen-sim/src/spawns.rs`. The first Free For All round on a map surveys it once for spots, every 13 cm across the counter and the decks its ramps lead up to (and the map's starts): a spot keeps a hotdog's whole body clear of the blocks, off the ramps and the sinks, on level ground and 10 cm from the counter's edge, and a hotdog put there faces the most open way, leaning towards the middle of the map. A round opens with the hotdogs spread as far apart as the map allows: one spot drawn at random, each next the spot farthest from those already taken, and which hotdog stands where drawn too, so that rounds open differently (on Counter Siege the closest two of the five are 0.92 m apart or more, where two of its starts are 0.55 m apart). A hotdog that dies is back five seconds later at a safe spot, with a second of spawn protection that its first shot or blow ends: none of the living within 0.9 m of it (as far as a bot walks in the second of protection, and a bite) or able to see it, as far from them as can be (up to 1.5 m counts), and away from where it died and where it last came back, drawn at random from the best three; with nowhere safe, it is back at the spot furthest from the living. The draws come from the game's seed, so the same seed gives the same spawns; online the server makes them and the frames say where everyone is. The first to 25 kills wins, or whoever has most when five minutes are up (a tie at the top is a draw); the score is shown for eight seconds and everyone is back in the fridge, as after a siege. Somebody arriving during a round plays at once. The head-up display shows kills, deaths, the time left and the leader. Single player plays it with `?play&mode=ffa` (Enter, or eight seconds, for the next round); `?mp&mode=ffa&map=NAME` makes the page choose it when it leads the lobby.

**The sandbox** is for trying things out: `?sandbox` in the address, with `?play` (single player) or `?mp` (online, on a server started with `--dev`). There is no lobby, countdown, clock, wave, build phase or end: a siege map plays its free for all without hunger or pests, a Forts map its fight with the forcefield down and no bacon or stalemate cats, and a hotdog that dies is back half a second later. The first player to join an empty `--dev` server sets the sandbox up for everyone who joins after; when the last one leaves, the server's lobby game comes back. A server without `--dev` passes a sandbox request over (the page then plays the usual game). The address may also say:

| Parameter | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `weapons=mustard,sniper` | the guns a person's hotdog starts and respawns with: `mustard`/`pistol`, `ketchup`/`sniper`, `hotsauce`/`flame`, `relish`/`bazooka`, `bugspray`/`spray`, `syrup`/`rifle`, `mayo`, or numbers 1 to 7, or `all` (Y and the mouse wheel cycle them: Y and wheel-down forwards, wheel-up back, in slot order) |
| `ammo=inf` | rounds never run out (every magazine and reserve kept full; with `mode=ffa` too) |
| `god` | a person's hotdog takes no damage (the hit is still on its record) |
| `bots=N` | how many bots play, 0 to 5 (the rest of the seats stand empty) |
| `botmode=idle\|walk\|fight` (or `bots=idle`...) | bots stand still, walk to and fro at their start, or play (the default) |
| `zombies=on` | cats come out of the microwave again whenever the last is gone (off by default) |
| `field=on` | Forts: the forcefield stays up |
| `stim=inf` | a stim lasts until death and can always be taken |
| `respawn=here` | back where it fell rather than at its start |
| `skill=easy\|normal\|hard` | how well the bots aim (with or without the sandbox; see [bots](#bots)) |
| `mode=siege\|forts\|ffa\|cards`, `map=NAME` | the mode, and the map (a file of `assets/maps`, or the one being played where it can hold the mode, or else the mode's default); `ffa` adds Free For All's rules without its end, and `cards` lays [Fully Loaded](#fully-loaded)'s table on Counter Siege (the other keys mean nothing at it) |

A "SANDBOX" badge shows in the corner. **`/dev.html`** on the server composes these links (server and port, online or offline, every parameter) and shows each server's `/status` (which now says `dev` and `rules`).

**Tuning.** In the sandbox, F2, the backquote key or the badge's Tune button slides out a panel (`tune.js`; it frees the mouse, and works by touch) with every number a gun goes by (shot speed, gravity, round size, range, time between shots, burst, damage to hotdogs and to zombies, splash, recoil, reload time, the mayonnaise's flow) and the stim's and the walk's, each with its default, live while playing: single player changes its own round, online the server takes changes in a sandbox on a `--dev` server and tells every player (`{type:'tune',text}` in, `{type:'tuning',text,editable}` out; protocol 12 is unchanged, and older peers ignore the new messages and fields). The bots read the same numbers. "Copy settings" copies what differs from the defaults as `mustard.speed=1.1 sniper.speed=9`, which `hotdog-server --tune "mustard.speed=1.1,sniper.speed=9"` takes as the tuning every game on that server starts with (`crates/kitchen-sim/src/tune.rs` lists the keys).

## Fully Loaded

**Fully Loaded** is the fourth mode: a press-your-luck card game that the hotdogs play standing round a little card table on top of Counter Siege's microwave (`crates/kitchen-sim/src/cards.rs` holds its rules; `src/cards.rs` draws the table). There are no cats, no guns, no hunger and no HP: the hotdogs take turns to flip topping cards onto their hotdog, and a topping they already have ruins the hand. The lobby's leader picks it from the mode list beside Siege, Forts and Free For All; it is played on Counter Siege only, so its map list is that one map (a map chosen on its own while it is chosen, from a link or the address, is played in its own mode; asking for Fully Loaded with another map is refused). When the fridge opens everyone goes straight to the table, and bots take the seats nobody has. Somebody who leaves during a game is replaced by a bot that keeps their score, and somebody who arrives waits in the fridge for the next game. The winner is shown for eight seconds, then everyone is back in the fridge. Chat and voice work at the table as they do in a round.

Single player plays it with four bots: `?play&mode=cards` in a browser, natively `--skip-intro --rules mode=cards` (a new game follows eight seconds after the winner, or at Enter). The demo (`?demo&mode=cards`, `--demo --rules mode=cards`) only watches: all five hotdogs are bots, and no seat, prompt or button is the viewer's. `?mp&mode=cards` makes the page choose it when it leads the lobby, and a server started with `--mode cards` starts its lobby on it.

**The tutorial.** `?tutorial` in the address (natively `--tutorial`, or `tutorial` among the `--rules` words) makes single player's table a guided first hand against the bots (`src/cards/tutorial.rs`). It never starts by itself: players are taken to know the game. This player is dealt first and has the first go, and its cards come up in the lesson's order: Pickles dealt, then Ketchup, Fries, Back in the Fridge, and a second Ketchup for a flip too many. Each bot is set cards of its own (one goes bust on its first flip), and until the hand is over its cards show, and its bots think, half as long again as in a game (`tutorial::PACE`, 1.5). A box over the prompt line says what each step is about, and each step waits for this player: flip a card (a topping is worth its number); what the Ketchup is worth, and that the same topping twice is a bust; that a side never busts; whom to send Back in the Fridge (look at a hotdog and click, or press Esc and click a name); and, holding two toppings, to stop and bank. When the hand is over it says how a game is won (first to 100; seven different toppings is fully loaded), and a few seconds later the game goes on as any other. With the touch controls on it names their buttons (Flip, Stop, Pick and Me). A stop before the last step, or Back in the Fridge played on itself, passes over the steps that can no longer come. K skips the tutorial at any time, as does a click on its "Skip tutorial (K)" note with the pointer free; by touch, Stop skips it while it asks for a flip (its note says so). A browser still remembers a lesson seen (`localStorage`, `hotdog.cards.tutorial`), but whether one is given is only the address's to say. It is never given online, where the server deals and nobody's game should wait on one player's lesson, nor in the demo. On the rules' side it is `Table::with_order`, which deals a lesson's cards seat by seat before the deck's (those nobody drew go back into the deck when the hand ends), and `Table::set_pace`.

**The cards.** 94 of them, shuffled at the start of a game:

- **Toppings** (79). A topping is worth its value, and there are as many copies of it as it is worth, so the valuable ones are the common ones, and the risky ones: Plain Bun 0 (one copy), Celery Salt 1, Sport Peppers 2, Jalapeños 3, Bacon 4, Cheese 5, Chili 6, Sauerkraut 7, Pickles 8, Onions 9, Relish 10, Mustard 11 and Ketchup 12.
- **Sides** (6), one of each, which never ruin a hand: Fries +2, Chips +4, Pickle Spear +6, Soda +8, Onion Rings +10, and the **Foot-Long**, which doubles the hand's topping points.
- **Action cards** (9), three of each, played on a hotdog the one who drew it picks from those still in the hand (itself included; alone, itself): **Back in the Fridge** makes that hotdog stop and bank its hand now; **Pile It On** makes it flip three cards one after another (fewer, if it is ruined or fully loaded first; an action card flipped meanwhile is dealt with after the three, if it is still in, and a Napkin kept at once); a **Napkin** is kept, and wipes away one duplicate topping (both go to the discards) so that it does not ruin the hand. A hotdog holds one Napkin at most: a second goes to another hotdog still in the hand that has none, or else to the discards.

**A hand.** Each hand the dealer is the next seat round (the first seat deals the first hand). Starting with the seat after the dealer, every hotdog is dealt one card face up (an action card dealt is played at once), then the turns go round from the same seat: the hotdog whose go it is **flips** a card or **stops** and banks what it has. A hotdog that has stopped, been ruined or been sent back to the fridge sits the rest of the hand out; one still in plays again when its go comes round. A topping it already has, with no Napkin to wipe it away, **busts** it: the hand is worth nothing to it. **Seven different toppings** (the Plain Bun counts) is **fully loaded**: 15 points more, and the hand is over for everybody at once (the others keep what they have). Otherwise the hand ends when nobody is left in. A hand is worth its toppings' values added up (doubled by a Foot-Long), plus its sides, plus 15 if fully loaded; a bust is worth nothing. The cards played go to the discards when the hand ends, and when the deck runs out the discards are shuffled into a new one (the cards in front of the hotdogs stay where they are).

**Winning.** After a hand, once anybody's total is 100 or more, the highest total wins. A tie at the top plays one more hand between the tied, until one of them is ahead.

**The pace.** A card dealt or flipped shows for 0.7 seconds before play goes on. Online a person has 15 seconds for a go (then it stops) and 15 for a pick (then the bots' choice is made for it); single player has no limit. A bot thinks for 0.8 to 1.6 seconds: it stops when the chance that its next card ruins it is more than it will risk (it risks more holding a Napkin, less with more points in hand), or when its hand alone would win the game; it sends Back in the Fridge to the opponent with the most at stake (or itself, when it is the one most likely to bust and has a good hand), and Pile It On to the opponent still in with the most toppings (the likeliest to bust; the higher total breaks a tie, then the lower seat), or to itself if it is the only one in. The scores between hands show for 3 seconds.

**At the table** this player looks out from its own seat at the others standing round the green felt, its own hotdog not drawn. Every hotdog's cards lie face up in front of it, the draw pile is in the middle as high as the cards left in it, and a mustard bar marks whose go it is. Each card shows, in a window on its colour, a cartoon of its food: a bun, a celery-salt shaker, sport peppers, a jalapeño, a rasher of bacon, a wedge of cheese, a bowl of chili, a heap of sauerkraut, pickle slices, an onion, a spoonful of relish, a mustard bottle and a ketchup bottle for the toppings; fries, chips, a pickle spear, a soda and onion rings for the sides; a long hotdog for the Foot-Long; and a fridge, a pile of toppings and a checked napkin for the action cards, which fill the card. Its value is in its top-left and bottom-right corners (`+4` for a side, `×2` for the Foot-Long), both upright, so that a 6 never reads as a 9. The faces are drawn in code from simple shapes (`src/cards/faces.rs`), a few a frame as the table is first laid, and the table is dealt once all 22 are printed. The others turn to look at whoever's go or pick it is, a bust slumps its hotdog for a moment, and fully loaded and the winner cheer. The scoreboard is in the top-left corner, under the hand's number (a card) and the points to win (a crown): each hotdog's name in its colour, its points over its total, how it stands as an icon (a burst for a bust, a hand for stopped, a fridge, a tick for fully loaded, a crown for the winner), and its cards this hand as chips in their colours with their values (`12`, `+2`, `x2`, or an action card's icon), the current player's row highlighted. The name tags over the heads keep clear of it. This player's own cards are those in front of its seat (they are not shown again on the screen), and along the bottom the prompt shows what to do in icons (the left mouse button and a card to flip, the right and a hand to stop, and a clock with the seconds left; with the touch controls only the card and the hand, as their buttons say the rest), whom the table waits on (`Frank ...`), each hotdog's points when a hand is over (`+12`, `BUST`), and the winner under a crown.

- **The mouse.** Click the game to capture the mouse, which then looks round from the seat (70° either way, 35° up and down). On this player's go the left button flips and the right stops. The click that captures the mouse (the first one, or the one after Esc, the chat box or another window) only captures it: it never flips or picks. In a browser the game goes by whether the page really has the mouse, so a click after the browser let it go without the game hearing Esc (another tab or window) only captures it too.
- **Picking** who gets an action card: with the mouse captured, look at a hotdog (its tag lights up white) and click, or free the mouse with Esc and click its name on the scoreboard (the way to pick oneself; the prompt shows a target, the card to give and the left button).
- **Touch**: Flip and Stop buttons (Pick and Me while picking), and a drag to look round (see [touch](#touch)).

**Online** the server runs the table instead of the round: it deals, keeps the clock and scores, and plays the bots. Each game socket is sent the table as JSON text when it joins while there is one and whenever it changes, `{type:'cards',table}` (the table's view: each seat's cards, status, hand points, total, Napkin and whether a bot plays it; the dealer, the hand, whose go or pick it is, the seconds left of a person's decision, the cards left in the deck, whether the scores between hands are showing, the winner, the latest events and a version that moves with every change). A client sends `{type:'cards',action:'flip'}`, `{type:'cards',action:'stop'}` or `{type:'cards',action:'target',seat}` (the hotdog it picks, by seat). The seat that acts is always the sender's own; a command that is not that seat's go, or not well formed, changes nothing. The frames, protocol 12's binary format and the stages are as they were: what a frame says of Fully Loaded is its `rules` (`mode=cards`). See `crates/server/src/protocol.rs` and `hub.rs`.

## Kung Fu

**Kung Fu** is the fifth mode: no guns, no pickups and nothing to carry, only hands and feet, every hotdog against every other in a ring on the kitchen counter. The lobby's leader picks it from the mode list after Fully Loaded (its maps are those made for it: the dojo, or Random among them); single player plays it on its map, `?play&map=kung-fu-dojo` in a browser or natively `--skip-intro --map assets/maps/kung-fu-dojo.json`, and `--rules mode=kungfu` (`?play&mode=kungfu`) with no map named opens the dojo too (on a map that is not a Kung Fu map the map's own mode is played, with a note saying so). `?mp&mode=kungfu` makes the page choose it when it leads the lobby, and a server started with `--mode kungfu` starts on it. The rules are the simulation's (`crates/kitchen-sim/src/kungfu.rs`): the same offline and online, where the server runs them. The fighters keep the pace the mode was balanced at, not the faster walk of the other modes: in its stance a fighter walks 0.48 m/s (0.85 of the 0.57 m/s the hotdogs once walked, `kungfu::STANCE_PACE` being that share of today's 1.25 m/s), blocking 0.17 m/s, a bot at 0.34 m/s times its style's pace.

**The dojo** ([assets/maps/kung-fu-dojo.json](assets/maps/kung-fu-dojo.json)) is at true scale beside 15 cm hotdogs. The ring is a restaurant's bamboo steamer lid, 0.96 m across and 3 cm high (a step up), woven, with a red lacquered rim; its front is 10 cm from the counter's front edge. Four long chopsticks stand round it in white holders, strung together over head height with little paper pennants and a red paper lantern in the middle of each run, each lit warm. On its right a wok hangs as a gong in a frame of cooking chopsticks, with a wooden spoon to strike it; on its left the spectators' stand is a tower of three bamboo steamers, a step apart, with rice bowls round it; tea things stand along the back. Its eight starts stand round the ring, facing its middle (a round has five hotdogs, people and bots).

**Winning.** Health is 100. A hotdog knocked out (no health left, or **rung out**: off the ring, or off the counter, where a big enough blow can carry it) is a KO for whoever did it the most harm in that life (a ring-out, for whoever last hit it within five seconds), and comes back after three seconds at a start away from the others, safe for a moment. The first to 10 KOs wins, or the most KOs after four minutes. Bots fill the empty places, each with a style.

**The styles.** Seven, each with ten moves of its own (three light strikes that chain, two heavy strikes, two kicks, a jump attack, a crouching sweep and a special), on top of fifteen every hotdog shares (the stance, walking in it, the high and low blocks, the parry, dodges left and right, a back hop, the light and heavy flinch, a knockdown and getting up, the grab and being thrown, and a bow): 85 animations in all.

- **Tiger**: power. Slow, heavy claws and palm strikes that break a block, the hardest hits.
- **Crane**: reach. Long kicks from one leg, wing blocks, the longest reach.
- **Snake**: speed. Fast finger jabs from a low stance, short reach, quick recovery, combos of pokes.
- **Mantis**: counters. Hooking, trapping hands, grabs and throws, and the most from a parry.
- **Monkey**: acrobatics. Rolls, flips and tumbling attacks from odd heights, and the best dodge.
- **Drunken**: the unpredictable. A swaying stance, stumbling lunges and falls that are attacks, its timing seemingly random (each strike a little faster or slower: the same for everybody, offline and online).
- **Toad**: the iron body. Squat and wide in a low stance, belly bumps, double-palm shoves, heavy stomps and leaping body slams, the strongest hits and the slowest feet. Standing its ground (in its stance or blocking, nearly still, with chi to spend) its **iron body** takes ordinary blows from in front at a tenth of their damage, unshaken, and the striker recoils, stunned; each blow spends a little chi. It is beaten by a grab or a throw, a guard-breaking blow, a special, or a blow from behind, and it is down while the Toad itself attacks, dodges or reels. It is the hardest style to beat when it is used well, and has a clear way to beat it.

**Fighting.** Light strikes chain into one another and into the heavies and kicks while the move is still running; a press during a move is kept for a fifth of a second. In the air a strike is the style's jump attack, crouched it is its sweep. Every move has its timing (start-up, active, recovery), reach, arc, damage, push and stun, and blows are high, middle or low. Holding block stops high and middle blows standing and low ones crouched, at a cost to the guard, which wears down and comes back by itself; a block raised within 0.12 s of a blow landing is a **parry**, which staggers the striker. A **grab** beats a block and ends in a throw; a **dodge** (Shift, the way the stick or the keys point; a back hop with none) slips through strikes and grabs untouched for a moment. Every style's second heavy strike breaks a block, and so do Tiger's first heavy and second kick. Big hits knock a hotdog down; it is untouchable on the ground and as it gets up. **Chi** fills from landing blows and parries; full, the **special** (T, or both strikes together) is the style's signature, unblockable.

**The head-up display** shows no gun and no rounds. Bottom right: the style's animal (a tiger's head, a crane on one leg, a snake, a mantis, a monkey's face, a gourd, a toad), ringed while a Toad's iron body is up, under the chi meter, which glows gold when it is full with the special's key beside it. Bottom left: the health, and under it the guard while it is down. Top: the clock, this player's KOs as pips of the ten to win, and the leader's KOs under a crown. Right of the crosshair the combo's count pops with every hit of a chain; over it a small icon for a moment says a blow was blocked (crossed forearms), parried (an arrow turned back off an arm) or broke a guard (a split shield), bright when this player did it and red when it was done to it, and a ring when a blow bounced off a Toad. The only word is the style's name, for a moment, when it changes.

**Controls** (all of them rebindable in the menu's Kung Fu and Styles tabs; see [the menu](#menu--controls)):

| Action | Default | | Action | Default |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Light strike | Left button | | Dodge | Shift |
| Heavy strike | Right button | | Special (full chi) | T |
| Kick | F | | Next, previous style | Wheel down, wheel up |
| Block (hold) / parry (tap) | Q | | Tiger, Crane, Snake, Mantis | 1, 2, 3, 4 |
| Grab and throw | E | | Monkey, Drunken, Toad | 5, 6, 7 |
| Jump (a strike in the air: the jump attack) | Space | | Crouch (a strike: the sweep; block: low) | Ctrl or C |
| Third person, or back to first | V | | | |

**The view** is first person to begin with: this player's own forearms, fists and (when they come up) feet doing the move, and no gun. **V** puts the camera over the right shoulder, where the whole hotdog is seen fighting (the camera pulled in before a wall and kept above the counter, and jolted by the blows as first person is), and back again (`src/fight.rs`; the key is in the menu's Movement tab). Near the edge of the ring a small red icon of a fall off a ledge comes up round the crosshair on the side the edge is, stronger the nearer it is: off the ring is a KO. Online the client walks its own hotdog ahead of the server as everywhere, at the pace its move allows and carried by the move's lunge or dodge as the server will carry it, so a dodge is not pulled back when the server's word comes.

A style changes as soon as the hotdog is free to (not in the middle of a move). By touch the buttons are the fight's: **Punch** (held), **Heavy**, **Kick**, **Block** (held), **Grab**, **Dodge**, **Special** (lit when the chi is full), **Style** (the next one), **Jump** and **Crouch**.

**Sounds** (`src/fu.rs` plays them; the recordings are cut by `tools/build_kungfu_user_sounds.py`, which needs `ffmpeg`, numpy and scipy, from the twelve royalty-free Pixabay `.mp3` files in `assets/audio/` into mono 22050 Hz `assets/audio/kungfu/*.wav`, since the game plays wave files only, native and in the browser; the originals are untouched, and `THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES.md` lists them; `tools/build_kungfu_sounds.py` synthesises the 13 `assets/audio/fu-*.wav`, of which the whoosh, the parry's clack, the broken guard's crack, the grab's rustle, the throw's thump, the Toad's clang, the gong and the chime are still played): a whoosh as a strike starts (the synthesised fuller one, or the swing of a recorded punch, for a heavy strike, a jump attack, a sweep or a special), a recorded punch or body thump for a hit (picked at random each time, never the same one twice running, and a touch higher or lower in pitch, a heavier set for a big hit), a dull recorded thud for a blow blocked, a wooden clack for a parry, a crack and a rattle of metal for a broken guard, a rustle for a grab, a thump and a heavy hit for a throw landing, a thump and a grunt for a knockdown, a metallic clang for a blow off a Toad's iron body, the wok gong and a scream for every KO (the gong swings too), a kiai (a shout) as a heavy strike starts and a held shout for a special, a plucked pair of notes when this player's style changes and a chime when its chi fills. A sting opens the round (the swell and boom of the intro) and one closes it (a victory sting for this player's win, another for anyone else's), and on the dojo a quiet loop of bamboo woods plays all round. A fighter's shouts are limited so that eight fighters are not a wall of screaming: one fighter shouts at most once every 1.2 seconds, one more than 3.5 m from this player is not heard to, and no more than three voices begin in any second (this player's own always does, and a KO's scream is never dropped). The takes are the client's own luck: the simulation is not asked. Each sound is quieter the further off it is, and all of them sit under the guns. Nobody has listened to them: levels were set by number (K-weighted loudness and peak, per file, printed by the build script), so someone should hear the dojo once and adjust `Sound::volume` in `src/fu.rs`.

**Online** the server runs the fight. Protocol 12's frames and stages are as they were: a frame's `rules` says `mode=kungfu`, a hotdog's weapon number is its style + 1, and the fight itself (each fighter's style, move and time into it, block, stun, chi, guard and combo, and what happened: hits, blocks, parries, broken guards, grabs, knockdowns, KOs) comes as JSON text, `{type:'kungfu', ...}`. A client sends its keys as the usual commands (light strike FIRE, heavy SCOPE, kick MELEE, block GRAB held, grab THROW, dodge STIM, special PRONE, the style as the command's weapon).

**In the map format** two styles came with the dojo. `bamboo` is the tenth material: a deck of bamboo is **round** (a steamer: the ellipse inside its rect is its floor, in the game, the server and the editor alike), and a bamboo block no wider than 8 cm is a chopstick in its holder (a round bamboo deck with three or more of them round its rim is a ring, strung and lit). `gong` is the fourteenth counter thing: a wok gong facing its `facing` side. A map's `mode` may be `kungfu`. Maps using either style are version 2.

## Titan

**Titan** is the sixth mode, a boss fight: **one giant hotdog against all the others.** The titan is twelve hotdogs tall (1.8 m standing, 1.1 m crouched, 0.36 m round), slow and enormously strong; the small hotdogs, two to eight of them (five unless the rules say `smalls=N`; bots fill the empty places), are 15 cm tall and fast, and play by the [gun basics](#basics-every-gun-mode) exactly: the loadout, the pickups, reloading, F, a round in the face. Either side may be people or bots. It is played on its own map, [Titan's Kitchen](#the-arena-titans-kitchen), the kitchen floor at night. The lobby's leader picks it from the mode list after Kung Fu; single player plays it on its map, `?play&map=titans-kitchen` in a browser or natively `--skip-intro --map assets/maps/titans-kitchen.json` (`?play&mode=titan&titan=me` to be the giant yourself), and `?mp&mode=titan` makes the page choose it when it leads the lobby, and a server started with `--mode titan` starts on it (on Titan's Kitchen). Its rules' keys, offline in the page's address or `--rules`, and in a sandbox: `mode=titan`, `titan=bot|random|me|<hotdog id>` (who is the giant; the lobby writes the one it casts), `smalls=N` (2 to 8 small hotdogs, 5 unless asked). The rules are the simulation's (`crates/kitchen-sim/src/titan.rs`, numbers in its `TABLE` and constants, retuned by bot matches): the same offline and online, where the server runs them; the giant on screen, its animation, cameras, effects and sounds are `src/titan/`.

**Goals.** The small hotdogs win by bringing the titan's health to nothing: it starts with 12,500 and 4,550 more for every small hotdog (35,250 against five; the bar across the top is notched at every tenth). The titan wins by using up the reinforcements and killing everyone left, or by still standing when the five-minute clock runs out. A small hotdog that dies is back five seconds later at a start far from the giant, while the shared pool of **reinforcements** lasts: three for every small hotdog at the start, shown as pips at the top of the screen; once it is empty the dead stay dead (OUT). Score: a small hotdog gets a point for every point of damage it does the giant, 25 more for each hit on the weak spot and 100 for the killing blow; the giant gets 100 a kill.

**The giant's moves**, each with a wind-up you can see coming (its **tell**: the head-up display warns of every one, see [the head-up display](#the-head-up-display)), a time it strikes, a recovery and a cooldown:

| Move | Key (the titan's player) | Tell | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Walk | WASD | – | 0.9 m/s (0.5 crouched), turning slowly; it steps on small hotdogs in its way, hurting and shoving them |
| Charge | Shift | 0.65 s: it leans and digs in | runs at 2.4 m/s nearly straight ahead for up to 2.5 s, knocking aside with damage whoever is in its path; running into a wall, a table or a chair makes it stumble. Cooldown 7 s |
| Ground smash | right button | 0.95 s: both fists high | both fists on the floor and a shockwave along it to 1.7 m, heavy damage near and nothing at its edge, throwing hotdogs back and up; in the air at that moment you take only a fraction, in a den half; cover stops it as it stops a relish blast. Cooldown 5.5 s |
| Swat | left button | 0.5 s: the arm swung back | a wide sweep of the arm: standing it sweeps high (from a hand's height over the floor to its waist, so it misses a hotdog on the floor but not one on a box or a stool), crouched low, along the floor and under the tables. Cooldown about 1.5 s |
| Stomp / kick | F | 0.38 s: the foot raised | a foot down just in front of it; crouched, a low kick ahead. Cooldown about 1.3 s |
| Jump | Space | 0.4 s: it crouches | a short heavy hop (0.28 m) and a smaller shockwave where it lands. It cannot land on anything raised. Cooldown 4.5 s |
| Crouch | Ctrl or C (held) | – | lowers it to 1.1 m: slower, its swat low enough to reach under the table, its weak spot tucked down |

**The weak spot and the stagger.** Rounds hit the giant where they hit, on the body as it is drawn at that instant (walking, striking, falling): its legs take three quarters of the damage, its arms and hands three quarters too, its body all of it, its face one and a half times, and **the back of its head, the nape, three times**: the classic way to bring a giant down, and it is not easy. The weak spot is the pale patch on the back of its head, all of it and nothing else: a round on the pale patch is on the weak spot. It faces back and well down, under the overhang of its skull: the giant bowing its head (looking down) turns it to face straight back, to anyone behind it, and tipping its head back sinks it behind its shoulders; crouched it is tucked down behind them too. It takes climbing, flanking, or a teammate drawing its look. Damage to the **legs fills its stagger meter** (260 points of it within about eight seconds): full, the giant drops **to its knees** for about three seconds, unable to move or attack, its head down within easy reach and its nape open to everyone behind it; after that its legs need a while before they can be staggered again. A charge into a wall is a shorter stumble that opens it too. The display marks the bar when it kneels, and shows a gold target whenever its weak spot is open to you.

**Playing the giant.** You see from its eyes, 1.7 m over the floor, where the small hotdogs are specks, each marked; V puts the camera behind and above it. The floor shakes under your feet. Mind your back: the small hotdogs want to get behind you, and the bots will (a gold cue says when one has your nape, and from where). **Watching**: the demo on a Titan map (`?demo&map=titans-kitchen`) follows the giant from behind and above, cutting to a small hotdog for a few seconds when the giant hits or kills it (`src/director.rs`); a camera watching a small hotdog in a den (the demo's, the death camera's, a replay's FOLLOW) stays under its roof, low, looking out of its mouth. **Playing a small hotdog.** Spread out, shoot the legs to stagger it, get behind it and shoot the back of its head, jump the shockwave, step out of the charge's line, and use the cover and the spaces under the furniture, which it cannot get into: its low swat and its smash still reach their mouths.

**Bots.** A bot titan hunts sensibly: it picks targets, mixes its moves, charges down open lanes, smashes groups, turns on whoever gets behind it, and is not kited round a table forever; a hard one also reads where they are going, strikes with whichever blow catches the most of them, and keeps its charge for a lane its target will still be in. Small bots spread out, flank for the weak spot, shoot its legs, dodge the tells and use the cover and the dens. Their skill (`skill=easy|normal|hard`) applies to both sides.

**In the lobby** the leader also chooses who is the titan: **Auto** (a bot while fewer than three people play, otherwise a player drawn), **a bot**, **a random player**, **me**, or a player by name; it goes round between rounds, and everyone sees who it is, with TITAN in the ready queue, before they ready up (`titan=bot|random|me|<id>` in the rules offline).

### The arena: Titan's Kitchen

[assets/maps/titans-kitchen.json](assets/maps/titans-kitchen.json) (laid out by `tools/titans-kitchen.mjs`) is the same kitchen as the counter maps, seen from its floor at night, at true scale: 7.2 m by 6 m of tiles under a ceiling 2.7 m up, out of the giant's reach. Along the back wall the counter's cabinets (0.9 m, the counter maps' counter) with the sink under a window and an oven between them; the fridge on the front wall, standing wide open, its door out at a right angle (a panel to hide behind, and solid where it is drawn); a sideboard on legs against the left wall; a table with four chairs and a bar stool in the open. **Light**: moonlight through the window over the sink, falling across the floor and the table's legs; the open fridge's light spilling over the floor in front of it; a warm strip left on under the wall cupboards and the oven clock's green; and the night's dim blue over everything, so that at every graphics level the walls, cabinets and furniture stay dark blue against darker and the giant's shape reads against them (`floor.rs`, and in `visuals.rs` the room's lift of the theme's fill, `ROOM_AMBIENT`, by level).

- **Pillars**: the table's and the chairs' legs and the bar stool's; the giant must walk round the furniture, and can be lured into it charging.
- **Dens**, only small hotdogs fit: the **toe-kick** under the cabinets (0.17 m high, the whole length of both runs), **under the table** and **the chairs**, and **under the sideboard**, each open on more than one side, so none is a trap and none is safe: the giant swats low along the floor and smashes at their mouths.
- **Climbs** to the giant's knees: a **cardboard box** (0.4 m) up the pet's carpeted ramp, its bowls at the foot, and a **step stool** (0.2 and 0.38 m) up a big cookbook dropped against it and a tea towel hanging down it.
- **Pickups** spread so nobody camps: boxes of ammunition under the table, in the toe-kick and on the box; the ketchup sniper on top of the stool and the relish bazooka in the open in front of the fridge, where reaching them is a risk.
- The titan starts in the middle; the small hotdogs round the edge. The siege and Free For All can be played here too (it has cat spawn points), Forts and Kung Fu cannot.

In the map format (see [Maps](#maps)) the arena is a map made for Titan (`"mode": "titan"`, the only maps the mode plays on), whose first start is the titan's and the rest the small hotdogs', drawn as a floor (`"floor": true`), with **box props that have a base as its dens**: small hotdogs walk in under them, the giant cannot (it can reach their mouths). The fridge, the oven and the pet's bowl are kits of their own, and the box is the `card` style.

## Replays

Every game can be watched again: from any player's eyes, following a player, or from a camera that flies anywhere over the kitchen; at a quarter, half, normal or twice the speed, paused and a frame at a time; with the stats at any moment.

**What is recorded.** Not the inputs, but the stream a client is sent: every frame (protocol 12) and every text beside them (the card table, the griddles, Kung Fu, the reserves, the tuning), each with its moment. Played back through the online client's own code, a replay looks as the game did, whatever the simulation's tuning is by then, for online and single-player games alike. The file (`.replay`, `kitchen_sim::replay`, format 2; format 1 files from before players' own views play as they did) is a small header (the format, the protocol, the build that wrote it, the map's name and its whole text, the mode and rules, the players, when it began), then chunks of about five seconds, each beginning with a whole frame and the latest text of each kind, so that a seek decodes five seconds at most, and a summary (the duration, the winner, the final stats). Each block is zlib-compressed (miniz_oxide); chunks are written as they close, so a recording cut short (a server that crashed) plays up to where it ends. A file of another format is refused with a message that says so; one from another build plays, with a warning. About 70 to 90 KB a minute of five hotdogs.

**The server** records every match while anybody plays it (from the fridge opening until the lobby, another map or the last player leaving; nothing while nobody plays) from a spectator's view, into `replays/` in the root it serves, keeping the newest 50 (the oldest deleted first). `--replays DIR` puts them elsewhere and `--replays-keep N` keeps another number (0 records nothing). `GET /replays` lists them as JSON (`id`, `date`, `mode`, `map`, `players`, `duration`, `winner`, `size`, `complete`: the names are those the game shows over the hotdogs, nothing else of anybody) and `GET /replays/<id>` downloads one. The lobby page has a **Replays** list of them (Watch, Save).

**Single player** records its own games, round by round: natively into `replays/` beside the settings file (`~/.config/hotdog-simulator/replays/`, the newest 20 of its own recordings kept, nothing else in the folder touched; `--replays-dir DIR` or `HOTDOG_REPLAYS_DIR` names another folder, and `--no-record` or `HOTDOG_NO_RECORD=1` records nothing), in a browser into the page's own storage (the newest six, saved when a round ends and every 20 seconds while it lasts; each can be saved as a file). A small red dot in the top left corner says a round is being recorded. **Scripted and scratch runs** (screenshots, checks, a copy of the client built to try something) should pass `--no-record` (or `--replays-dir` into their own scratch folder, and `HOTDOG_SETTINGS` for the settings file) so that they never write into the player's own folders; a run with `HOTDOG_SHOT_DIR` set records nothing unless it is given `--replays-dir`. In a browser `?norecord` turns it off for the page. The Esc menu's **REPLAYS** lists them (and in a browser on the server, the server's matches), and its switch turns recording off (`RECORD REPLAYS`, on by default).

**Watching.** Natively `hotdog-simulator --replay FILE`; in a browser `/?replay=ID` (one of the server's), `/?replay=URL`, or a replay of the browser's own from the list. No server or network is needed for a file. The bar at the bottom has play and pause, the speeds, the time, and the timeline (click or drag it to seek), with the kills and Kung Fu knockouts marked in red and the ends of rounds in gold. Sounds are pitched with the speed, and silent while paused.

| key | does |
| --- | --- |
| P or K | play or pause (at the end, from the start again) |
| [ and ] (or - and =) | slower and faster: 0.25x, 0.5x, 1x, 2x |
| . | one frame on, paused |
| Left and Right | five seconds back and on |
| B and N | the kill before and after |
| V | first person, following, flying, in turn |
| Q and E (or Up and Down) | the previous and next player; 1 to 5 each by its place |
| W A S D, Space, Ctrl or C, Shift | fly (forward, left, back, right, up, down, fast); the wheel changes the flying speed (following: the distance) |
| drag (the right button; the left off the bar) | look round |
| Tab (or I) | the stats |
| T, Y, R, M | names over everyone, damage numbers, shot tracers, the map |
| U | hide everything on the screen, for clean footage |
| ? or F1 | every key of the viewer |

They are the Esc menu's controls tabs REPLAY and REPLAY CAMERA, rebindable; they are heard only in the viewer, so they may share the game's keys. In first person the watched player's own head-up display is up; following and flying, the screen has only the viewer's.

**Players' own views (RECORDED VIEW).** Beside the neutral stream, a replay can hold, for any player, what that player's own screen showed (`kitchen_sim::pov`, written by `src/replay/pov.rs`): for each frame it drew (at most 120 a second), on the match's clock, the camera exactly as it was drawn (after the recoil, the shakes, the scope, a third-person or death camera) and its field of view, which view it was, the first-person gun as it was drawn (which one, its place and turn in the view), and what the display reads of the player (its predicted place and look, stance, health, every gun's magazine and reserve, the aim, the kill cross, the reload, the stim, the rifle's burst); with the frame's length, the real clock and the buttons held and the mouse's move for diagnosis; and what happened on each frame: shots, reloads begun, cut short or finished, a change of gun, the scope up or down, melee, Kung Fu moves, the hit and kill markers and the damage indicators as that screen heard them, kill slow motion, relish on the lens, pickups, carrying, the graphics level, and the seed of single player's card shuffle (nothing else on the screen is random). A header says which build, renderer, graphics level and window drew it and the settings that change the picture (field of view, scope held or toggled). Each frame is a set of quantised channels (positions to 1/65536 m, turns to a millionth of a turn, the real clock exactly as the game read it), predicted from the frames before and kept as the differences, in batches of about a second, zlib-compressed: about 30 KB a minute at 30 frames a second, 45 KB at 60, 70 to 80 KB at 120 or more.

* **Single player** keeps its view in the round's own recording, as another track of the file.
* **Online** the client keeps its own copy (natively `<date>-<map>-view.replay` in the replays folder, the newest 20 kept; in a browser in its storage, the newest six), and, with **SHARE MY VIEW IN MATCH RECORDINGS** on (the Esc menu's REPLAYS page; on to begin with), sends each batch to the server as a `{"type":"pov"}` text beside its commands; a server that does not know it passes it over, and protocol 12's frames are as they were. The server keeps each player's view in that match's recording, and never plays it: it checks every batch (its size, that it reads whole, its clock running forward within the match, its frames within the rate), takes at most 4 batches a second and 300 KB a minute of match from a player (32 MB in all), and drops a view that breaks a rule for the rest of that recording, with a note of why; the player plays on. `GET /replays` lists whose views each recording holds (`views`). Natively, a server's recording without a player's view is watched through the copy this client kept of it, when it has one.
* `--no-pov` or `HOTDOG_NO_POV=1` (in a browser `?nopov`) keeps no view at all; the local copy, a file, follows `--no-record`, `--replays-dir` and `HOTDOG_SHOT_DIR` as recordings do.

Watching a player in first person whose view the replay holds, the bar says **RECORDED VIEW**: the camera is exactly where that player's was (between two of its frames when the replay's frames fall between them, so at any speed and after a seek), the gun in hand as it was drawn, and what the display reads is put back so that the game's own code draws the rest as it drew it then: the scope and the rifle's optic (its lens, reticle and readout), the crosshair and the kill cross, the display, the reload, the stim, Kung Fu's hands; the damage indicators, the hit marker and the slow motion's look (for the screen only: the replay goes on at its speed) hear what that screen heard, on that player's own clock. A seek hears the last few seconds again, each at its moment. Otherwise (bots, format-1 files, players who did not share) the bar says **RECONSTRUCTED**: the view from the frames, now with the gun in hand, kicked by the shots and swung by the swings the frames tell of, and the scope up when the frames say the player aimed. What a recorded view cannot make identical: a viewer at another graphics level or window shape draws the same view differently; what the client draws from its own clock alone (idle animations, the microwave's glow, particles) and anything waiting on assets or shaders loading; the other hotdogs and the rounds in the air, drawn from the recording's 30 frames a second rather than as that client drew them (single player's own round right after a shot is the plainest case); local animations during single player's slow motion, which in a replay go at the replay's pace; stains from before a seek's keyframe (not rebuilt); and the sounds, heard from the replay's own events.

**The stats** (Tab) are the scoreboard at the moment watched (kills, deaths, score) and, for each player, health, the weapon in hand with its magazine and reserve, accuracy (hits on other hotdogs for every round fired or swing made), damage dealt and taken, and the kill streak (and the best). They are worked out from the frames: a hit is a new record of the last damage a hotdog took, so two hits on one hotdog within a frame (1/30 s) count as one, and damage to the cats, which a frame does not attribute, is not counted.

## Map editor

The server serves the editor: open <http://127.0.0.1:8137/editor/> (with `--host 0.0.0.0`, from other machines too). It is read-only and serves only what the game and the editor load: the editor, the shared map rules ([tools/network/map.mjs](tools/network/map.mjs)) and the loose objects' shapes ([tools/network/presets.mjs](tools/network/presets.mjs)), the maps in `assets/maps/` and their list at `/assets/maps/`, and the game (the page with its lobby and touch-control scripts and styles, both browser builds and `assets/`, except the personal source images that are kept out of Git). The editor's Play button opens the game on the map being edited, and the game address in the editor's Counter panel can point it at a game served elsewhere.

The editor opens [assets/maps/counter-siege.json](assets/maps/counter-siege.json) and keeps an unsaved draft in the browser. Open lists every map in `assets/maps/`, and a link such as `/editor/?map=counter-siege-v2` opens one directly. The 3D view beside the plan follows every edit as it is made. Draw blocks, decks, ramps and props, and place hotdog starts, zombie spawns, weapon and ammunition pickups and turrets on the plan (the pickup tool's Holds field chooses a gun or an ammo box: the plan draws a box as an olive crate with three brass rounds, a gun as a diamond in its colour, and the inspector says what it gives and how long it takes to come back: the game's rules, the same on every map); the panel on the right edits the selection, and the problems list reports starts, spawns, pickups and decks that cannot be reached. A map that sets its own pickup times (`respawn`, from before) keeps them in its file, passed over by the game; the Counter panel's button takes them out. Download writes the map as canonical JSON, byte for byte the text the server sends its clients (`Map::to_text` in `crates/kitchen-sim/src/map.rs`). The format, collision and navigation rules live in [tools/network/map.mjs](tools/network/map.mjs), and `map.rs` keeps the same rules for the game and the server.

## Maps

The arena is a map in the editor's format: the simulation, the 3D scene and the multiplayer server all build the counter, cover, appliances, decks, ramps, turrets, starts, zombie spawns and pickups from it. Without one the game plays the bundled [assets/maps/counter-siege.json](assets/maps/counter-siege.json).

- Native: `--map <file>`, for example `--skip-intro --map assets/maps/counter-siege-v2.json`. An unreadable or invalid map prints the reason and exits with status 1.
- Browser: `?map=<name>` plays `assets/maps/<name>.json`, for example <http://localhost:8137/?play&map=counter-siege-v2>.
- Editor: Play opens the game on the map being edited, and while that tab stays open every edit rebuilds the arena and restarts the round without reloading the page; a hotdog being played keeps its place when that spot still exists. A map the game cannot read leaves the game as it is and shows the reason in a banner on the page; at startup the bundled map is played instead.
- Multiplayer: the server's map is the one played, whatever the page says: the lobby's leader chooses it from `assets/maps` (or Random) for each round, and the server's `--map` and `--mode` say what it starts on. A map put in `assets/maps` while the server runs can be chosen once the lobby is back in the fridge.

The map's `theme` is available to the scene as the `level::MapTheme` resource. Everything built from a map carries the `level::MapPart` component.

**Modes and maps.** The mode is the game's; each map can be played in the modes it can hold (kitchen-sim `Map::refuses`, the lobby greys the rest with the reason):

### The kitchen

The room round the counter (floor, ceiling, walls, the cupboards and worktop under the back of the counter and the cupboards on the wall above it) is sized from the map's bounds (`fridge::Room`). The bundled counter, 3.6 m by 1.36 m, gets the room the game always had: side walls at x = ±2.5, the back wall at z = 3.7. A longer or deeper counter pushes the walls out, keeping them 0.7 m past its ends and just behind its back, and the run of cupboards grows to its length; the room never shrinks below the bundled one. It grows away from the fridge, which stays where it is, in the open front of the room at x = 0, facing the middle of the counter some two metres off (2.2 m from the front of the bundled Forts counter). The largest kitchen holds a counter from x = -4.5 to 4.5 reaching back to z = 5 (`ROOM` in `tools/network/map.mjs` and `crates/kitchen-sim/src/map.rs`): walls at x = ±5.2 and z = 5.07. A counter bigger than that pokes through the walls, and the editor's problems list says so. When the editor changes a map's bounds the room is built again at the new size. The sun's shadows reach as far across a bigger counter as across the bundled one.

### Pickups in a map

A pickup is `{"p": [x, z], "weapon": w}`: `weapon` 1 to 7 is a gun (the mustard pistol, the ketchup sniper, the hot sauce thrower, the relish bazooka, the bug spray, the syrup rifle, the mayo squirter), and `weapon` 0 is a box of ammunition, written `{"p": [x, z], "weapon": 0}` (`WEAPONS`, `AMMO_BOX` in `tools/network/map.mjs`; `kitchen_sim::map`). Every pickup is a spawn point (see [pickups](#pickups)). What a gun's pickup gives and how long any pickup takes to come back are the game's rules, the same on every map: a gun's pickup may still say `"ammo": n` (1 to 999), and a map `"respawn": [gun seconds, ammo seconds]` (each 1 to 600, written after `build`), as older maps do; both are read and written back unchanged, and passed over. The editor writes a new gun's full load as its `ammo`, so builds from before still read the map.

and the game's older ones: a `bowl` (the big silver baking bowl, 32 cm across), a `crate`, a `pillow` (14 by 10 cm and 4 cm thick, nearly square-edged, gripping and dead, quick to sleep: made to pile up), a `chair` and a `tray`, which the siege maps put out (the Forts maps have only the kitchen's pieces). A `"kind": "blanket"` in an old map is read as a napkin. The bundled map has one bowl. What the map puts out at rest (on the counter, a deck or one another) starts asleep, and what stands in a pile starts locked (see [carrying](#carrying)); the hints call each by its name (`map::LOOSE_NAMES`: a cereal box, a mug, a tin can, a cutting board, a cereal bowl, a paper towel roll, a salt shaker, a pepper shaker, a bread bin). The game draws the kitchen's pieces itself, at their true size (`src/loose.rs`): the cereal box printed on every face (a bowl of flakes under a rising sun on orange card), the cutting board's beech grain, juice groove and the handle slot cut through it, a teal mug with its handle and the coffee in it, the plate's cobalt rings and unglazed foot, the cereal bowl's speckled sage glaze, white inside, the tin's label and ridged rims, the roll of paper towels quilted on its side and wound in rings on its ends round its cardboard core, a shaker's glass with the salt (or the pepper) in it and its steel cap with its holes, the bread bin's roll top and BREAD on its lip, and the napkin's red and white gingham. They are rigid bodies (`crates/kitchen-sim/src/props.rs`, under the bodies' gravity), drawn round the same outlines the physics uses; the map editor draws them from [tools/network/presets.mjs](tools/network/presets.mjs), which a test holds to the same parts.

- Hotdogs and zombie cats are stopped by them and shove them along (not one that is locked); a hotdog can stand in the bowl, and stepping on its rim tips it.
- A round stops at a solid loose object and nudges it, and goes through a light one (a cereal box, a roll of paper towels, a pillow), slowed and weakened, nudging it a little (see [materials and rounds](#materials-and-rounds)). A solid loose object is cover wherever a wall would be (shots, a glob's splash, what a bot can see, and where Free For All brings a hotdog back out of sight); any loose object, light or solid, stops the melee strike and the turret's beam.
- A relish glob's burst throws the loose objects around it, from the same point and with the same strength as it throws bodies.
- One pushed or thrown over the counter's edge falls to the kitchen floor and is out of the round. A new round puts every one back where the map has it (and takes every drape away).
- A napkin put or thrown over others, or over an edge, drapes: see [draping a napkin](#draping-a-napkin).
- **A napkin is drawn as cloth** (`src/cloth.rs`), never as the flat pad it is in the physics, which stays the authority for where it is, what it hits, who carries it and where it drapes. The cloth is a sheet of 12 by 12 particles (144, 4.1 cm apart) joined to their neighbours, across each square and to the next but one, stepped 120 times a second by Verlet integration with four passes of constraints, and drawn twice as fine by curves through the particles, both faces shown, in a red and white gingham with a darker hem. Carried, it hangs from the carrier's two hands (in first person from two fists low in the view), sways and trails as the carrier walks, turns and jumps, and lies over what it trails on; thrown or dropped, the few particles round its middle follow the pad and the rest flies against air that resists motion across the sheet, so it billows and flutters as it falls, and then settles over whatever it lands on (the counter, a deck or a ramp, a block's edge, other loose objects, the edge of the counter, which it hangs over or falls past to the kitchen floor). It collides with the counter, the map's blocks, decks and ramps, the draped walls and the forcefield, the other loose objects' own shapes and the hotdogs' bodies (a hotdog walks over cloth lying on the counter), with friction, so a napkin keeps its folds; once still it sleeps and costs nothing until its pad moves or something comes near. Six napkins all moving at once cost well under 2 ms a frame in a native release build (the client's tests hold it under 2 ms). Draped, the wall's own sheet is drawn instead (laid over the tops of what it covers, its sides hanging to the counter in folds). Offline and online alike the cloth is driven by the client's own copy of the pads.
- A hard impact rings (the bowl, the tray, a tin, the bread bin), chimes high (china and glass: a plate, a mug, a cereal bowl, a shaker) or knocks (wood: a crate, a chair, a cutting board), louder the harder it is; cloth, card and paper make no sound.

Each step moves the loose objects first, by what was done to them since the last step, then the bodies, each of which is put outside every loose object as it goes.

**What a package holds.** The game (`hotdog-simulator`) and the server (`hotdog-server`) (in the Linux and Windows packages, launchers that start one of two builds in `bin/`: see Processors below), the assets the game loads, `README.md`, `LICENSE`, `COPYRIGHT`, `THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES.md`, `THIRD_PARTY_LICENSES.html` ([below](#third-party-licences)) and `HOW-TO-RUN.txt` (starting it, hosting a server, where the settings live, the licence and where the source is). The game finds `assets/` beside its program or beside the `bin/` it is in (in the Mac app, in `Contents/Resources`), and the server serves the folder it came in, from any working directory; nothing needs `BEVY_ASSET_ROOT`. The assets are what `src/` names (the sounds, shaders, models and the package label), the food textures of the build's kind (PNG natively, WebP in a browser) and every map (`tools/dist-common.sh`, `dist_asset_list`): not the original MP3 downloads in `assets/audio/` (the game plays the cuts in `assets/audio/kungfu/`) and not `assets/models/`. The [breakfast intro's](#the-breakfast-intro) people are made in code and need no file, so every package plays the intro; `assets/CHARACTER_CREDITS.md` says how they are made. The browser package also has the page, its scripts, the map editor and both builds.

**The version.** Every package is named by one version, Cargo.toml's with the commit's short hash and `.dirty` when the working tree differs from the commit (`hotdog-simulator-0.1.0-74c7c92-linux-x86_64.tar.gz`); the game logs it at start (`Hotdog Simulator 0.1.0+74c7c92`), and the Windows `.exe` and the Mac app carry it in their properties. `HOTDOG_VERSION` sets another.

**Processors.** The Linux and Windows packages carry the game and the server twice, in `bin/`: compiled for x86-64-v3 (AVX2, FMA and BMI2: Intel from 2013, AMD from 2015) and for x86-64-v2 (SSE4.2, SSSE3 and POPCNT: every x86-64 processor from about 2009 to 2011). What a player starts, `hotdog-simulator` or `hotdog-server`, is a small launcher (`crates/launcher`, compiled for plain x86-64) that asks the processor and starts the v3 build when it can and the v2 build otherwise, with the same arguments and its exit code (on Linux it becomes the program; on Windows the game's launcher opens no console and the server's shares its console). `--cpu v2|v3` or `HOTDOG_CPU=v2|v3` forces one, and the game's log says which runs and why (`Hotdog Simulator 0.1.0+74c7c92, the x86-64-v3 build, chosen by the launcher: x86-64-v3, the best this processor runs`). The assets are one copy for both. Each build also checks at start, with `cpuid`, that the processor has what it was compiled for, and stops with a message (on Windows in a message box) instead of an illegal instruction when it is started directly on one that does not. `CPU=native|v3|v2|baseline tools/build-linux.sh` (or `build-windows.sh`) makes a one-off build of one level instead, the programs at the top of the folder and no launcher: `CPU=native` is the fastest build for the machine that builds it, for playing there. The level is passed as `-C target-cpu` for that one target triple in the script's own environment (`tools/dist-common.sh`, `dist_cpu`), so the browser builds and every other build are left alone. The Mac app's Intel half is x86-64-v2 only (`MAC_X86_CPU`): a second Intel build and a launcher inside the app were not worth it for Intel Macs, and v3 alone would leave out the 2013 Mac Pro, which macOS 11 and 12 still support; Apple Silicon has NEON, its only SIMD, always. The simulation agrees with itself bit for bit at every level (glam fuses multiply-adds only with its `fast-math` feature, which is off, and the simulation calls no `mul_add`): a minute of bots on Counter Siege, Pillow Fort, the whole kitchen's Forts, the Kung Fu dojo and a 128-cat horde steps to the same bits at baseline, v2 and v3, the simulation's and the server's tests give the same results at each, and Kung Fu's balance tables are identical. The client's prediction of its own hotdog (`Feast::predict`, 20 s of walking, crouching and jumping) gives the same bits at every level too, so a v3 game predicts a v2 server exactly, and the reverse. Measured on a Ryzen 9 5950X shared with other builds (the median of six interleaved runs), a simulation step on the whole kitchen's Forts map took 0.27 ms at baseline, 0.23 at v2 and 0.22 at v3; on Pillow Fort 0.17, 0.14 and 0.135; with the 128-cat horde 0.048, 0.044 and 0.041. So v2 gains 10 to 20% over baseline and v3 another 1 to 5%. The game's frames are held to the display's 60 Hz, and no difference between the levels could be told apart from the noise there.

**Toolchains.** Each platform builds in a target directory of its own (`target/dist-linux-v2`, …; `TARGET_PREFIX` moves them), and every script stops when the disk has less than 25 GB free (`MIN_FREE_GB`). The packages were first built with the nightly toolchain of 2026-10-04 and these, unpacked in a user folder (no installation):

- [zig 0.17.1](https://ziglang.org/download/) and [cargo-zigbuild 0.24.1](https://github.com/rust-cross/cargo-zigbuild/releases), named by `ZIG` and `CARGO_ZIGBUILD` or found on the `PATH` (`pip install ziglang cargo-zigbuild` installs both). Without them the Linux package needs the building machine's own glibc or newer (2.43 on Ubuntu 26.04: only the newest distributions).
- [llvm-mingw 20261104](https://github.com/mstorsjo/llvm-mingw/releases), the UCRT build for Linux, named by `LLVM_MINGW`. It is free (LLVM's licence and mingw-w64's); no Microsoft SDK is downloaded. On Windows itself the script uses the installed Visual Studio tools (`x86_64-pc-windows-msvc`) instead.
- For the browser package, what `tools/build-web.sh` needs ([Browser](#browser)).

**macOS from Linux.** It cannot be done legitimately: the game links against Apple's frameworks (Metal, AppKit, CoreAudio), which means the macOS SDK, and Apple licenses that for use on Apple hardware only. `tools/build-mac.sh` runs on a Mac (Xcode or its command line tools): it builds both architectures, joins them with `lipo`, makes `Hotdog Simulator.app` (Info.plist, the icon, the assets in `Contents/Resources`), signs it ad hoc, and makes the `.dmg` and `.zip`. To spare people Gatekeeper's warning the app needs a Developer ID certificate (an Apple Developer Program membership) and Apple's notarisation: `MAC_SIGN_IDENTITY` and `MAC_NOTARY_PROFILE` (see the script) do both.

**On GitHub.** [.github/workflows/release.yml](.github/workflows/release.yml) builds every package on its own platform's runner (Linux on Ubuntu 22.04 with zig, Windows natively with MSVC, both Mac architectures on a macOS runner, and the browser package) when a tag `v*` is pushed or when it is run by hand, and uploads them as the run's artifacts. It signs and notarises the Mac app when the repository has the secrets it names. Every package has the breakfast intro (there is no asset to leave out).

The icon is drawn by [tools/packaging/make_icon.py](tools/packaging/make_icon.py) (`hotdog.png`, `hotdog.ico`, `hotdog.icns`).

### Third-party licences

`THIRD_PARTY_LICENSES.html` (about 450 KB, committed at the repository's root) is the licence notice of every build: the game's own licence and where its source is, the licence text of each of the Rust crates (over five hundred) the programs and the browser builds are made of (once per distinct text, with the crates it covers), the fonts (Fira Mono, Cantarell, the editor's Barlow and Fredoka), the Bevy shader code and lookup tables, `vendor/wgpu-hal`, the gamepad database and the Pixabay clips' credits, and the full GPL. Every package carries it (`tools/dist-common.sh`, `dist_stage_docs`; in the Mac app also in `Contents/Resources`), the browser package puts it in its root, where `hotdog-server` serves it at `/THIRD_PARTY_LICENSES.html`, and the page, the lobby and the Esc menu link to it. It is made by `tools/build-licenses.sh` (cargo-about 0.9.3, `about.toml`, `tools/licenses/render.py`, and what is not a crate by hand in `tools/licenses/other.json` with the texts beside it): run it after a dependency changes and commit the result. It fails when a crate has a licence `about.toml` does not accept (they are all permissive, and compatible with GPL-3.0-only; a copyleft or unknown one is to be read before it is added) or none it can read. `tools/build-licenses.sh --check` makes it again somewhere else and fails if the committed file differs; CI runs that, and `tools/licenses.test.mjs` tests the rest. The address of the source is the `repository` line of `Cargo.toml`, the one place it is written (`HOTDOG_SOURCE_URL` overrides it); `HOW-TO-RUN.txt` states the licence, that address and the commit of the build.

## Continuous integration and deployment

The game is hosted at **https://hotdog-simulator.chilos.dev**: one `hotdog-server` on the server, behind an HTTPS proxy (browsers give WebGPU to secure pages only), serving the page, both browser builds, the map editor, `/status`, `/replays` and the WebSockets. Two workflows look after it, beside [release.yml](.github/workflows/release.yml) (which keeps building every package, the macOS one included, on a `v*` tag or by hand; deploying builds only the Linux and web packages it deploys):

- **[ci.yml](.github/workflows/ci.yml)**, on every pull request and every push to a branch: the [checks](#checks) above, in four jobs at once (the simulation, its C interface, the server and the launchers; the client's tests; both browser builds type-checked; the Node tests), with the toolchain release.yml pins (nightly-2026-10-04). One status sums them up, **`ci-ok`**: the one to require. The tests that time a step, a plan or a query against a budget would fail on a shared runner's slower, noisier processor, so ci.yml sets `HOTDOG_TIMING_SLACK=3`, which multiplies each such budget (`kitchen_sim::timing_slack`; unset, as on a developer's machine, they are the strict ones: set it yourself to run the suites on a slow or loaded machine). The one that has failed on a busy machine, the simulation's `the_bots_building_costs_little_per_step`, is also run on its own after the rest of its suite by [tools/ci/cargo-test-retry.sh](tools/ci/cargo-test-retry.sh) and given one retry, with a warning on the run; a second failure fails, and nothing else is retried. Only main's runs save the build caches, which every branch then starts from.
- **[deploy.yml](.github/workflows/deploy.yml)**, on every push to main (a merged pull request) or by hand (Actions > deploy > Run workflow, with any commit of main): the tests (ci.yml, called for that very commit, so a merge is tested once and only a tested commit is deployed), the Linux and web packages built meanwhile by `tools/build-all.sh` (kept as the run's artifacts for two weeks), then the **deploy** job, in the GitHub environment `production`, one at a time, which runs [tools/deploy/deploy.sh](tools/deploy/deploy.sh) over SSH; then a smoke test, headless Chrome playing a round from the public page ([tools/check_tab_switch.mjs](tools/check_tab_switch.mjs), drawn in software since a runner has no GPU), which never fails the run. A run whose commit main has already moved past deploys nothing (the newer commit's run does). Until the settings below exist the deploy job only says so and passes.

**On the server** ([tools/deploy/](tools/deploy/)): `DEPLOY_PATH/releases/<UTC time>-<version>/` each hold `server/` (the Linux package's `hotdog-server` launcher and its v2 and v3 builds) and `web/` (the web package); `current` points at the live one; `replays/` holds the recorded matches, which no deploy touches. [hotdog-simulator.service](tools/deploy/hotdog-simulator.service) runs `current/server/hotdog-server --host 127.0.0.1 --port 8150 --root web --replays DEPLOY_PATH/replays` (no `--dev`), restarted if it fails, hardened (no new privileges, a system-call filter, and in a system service a read-only file system but for the replays). A deploy uploads the release, unpacks and checks it (its server must run there), points `current` at it atomically and restarts the service, then checks it from the server and at the public address: the page, both builds' `pkg/` and `pkg-webgpu/` files (`application/wasm`, the brotli copy passed on byte for byte, `no-cache`), `/status`, `/replays` and a WebSocket upgrade at `/ws` and `/lobby`. If any check fails it points `current` back at the release before, restarts, deletes the failed release and fails loudly; otherwise it keeps the newest five releases. A restart ends the games in progress: their players land back in the lobby.

**Headers.** The server sets what the game needs itself: `application/wasm` for the modules, `Cache-Control: no-cache` with an ETag on every file (the bundle's names carry no hash, so a browser asks again each load and gets a 304 while its copy is current, and a deploy is picked up at once), and the precompressed `.br`/`.gz` copies of the builds with `Content-Encoding`, which neither proxy compresses again. Nothing needs cross-origin isolation (no `SharedArrayBuffer` or WebAssembly threads). The proxy examples add HSTS and nothing that caches.

### Setting it up, once

1. **The repository.** Create it on GitHub and push `main` (and any branch): `git remote add origin git@github.com:OWNER/hotdog-simulator.git && git push -u origin main`.
2. **The server's account.** As root on the server, an unprivileged account for the game, which the deploys log in as (no sudo): `adduser --disabled-password --gecos '' hotdog`.
3. **A deploy key**, on your own machine: `ssh-keygen -t ed25519 -N '' -C hotdog-simulator-deploy -f hotdog-deploy`. The private half (`hotdog-deploy`) becomes the GitHub secret `DEPLOY_SSH_KEY`; the public half (`hotdog-deploy.pub`) goes to the server in the next step. Keep the private file only if you will deploy or roll back by hand.
4. **The server's setup**, as that account (it needs no root and can be run again):
   ```sh
   scp tools/deploy/remote-setup.sh tools/deploy/hotdog-simulator.service hotdog@hotdog-simulator.chilos.dev:
   ssh hotdog@hotdog-simulator.chilos.dev ./remote-setup.sh --authorize "$(cat hotdog-deploy.pub)"
   ```
   It checks the tools a deploy uses (`tar`, `gzip`, `flock`, `curl`, `systemctl`), makes `~/hotdog-simulator/{releases,incoming,replays}` (`--path` for another place), installs the user service, adds the key to `authorized_keys` restricted to running commands, turns on lingering if it may, and prints what root must still do: usually `loginctl enable-linger hotdog`. `--system` instead writes a system unit for root to install, with a sudoers line that lets the account restart that one service; set the variable `DEPLOY_SCOPE=system` then. (In a user service the file-system sandbox needs unprivileged user namespaces, which Ubuntu 24.04 and later restrict; systemd then quietly does without it. A system service always has it.)
5. **The HTTPS proxy**, as root: [Caddyfile.example](tools/deploy/Caddyfile.example) (Caddy gets and renews the certificate for `hotdog-simulator.chilos.dev` by itself) or [nginx.conf.example](tools/deploy/nginx.conf.example) (with certbot; the steps are in it). Open ports 80 and 443 and nothing else of the game's: the server listens on 127.0.0.1:8150 only.
6. **The server's host key**, pinned: `ssh-keyscan -p 22 hotdog-simulator.chilos.dev > known_hosts`, then compare `ssh-keygen -lf known_hosts` with what the server itself says (`ssh-keygen -lf /etc/ssh/ssh_host_ed25519_key.pub` there). The file's lines become the secret `DEPLOY_KNOWN_HOSTS`: deploys accept that key and no other.
7. **The settings**, in Settings > Secrets and variables > Actions (or in Settings > Environments > production, which the first run creates; there you can also add required reviewers, so that each deploy waits for a click, and limit deployment to the `main` branch):

   | Name | Kind | Value |
   | --- | --- | --- |
   | `DEPLOY_SSH_KEY` | secret | the deploy key's private half |
   | `DEPLOY_KNOWN_HOSTS` | secret | the server's host key lines (step 6) |
   | `DEPLOY_HOST` | variable | `hotdog-simulator.chilos.dev` (or the server's IP) |
   | `DEPLOY_USER` | variable | `hotdog` |
   | `DEPLOY_PATH` | variable | `~/hotdog-simulator` (or the `--path` given to remote-setup.sh) |
   | `DEPLOY_PORT` | variable, optional | the SSH port, 22 by default |
   | `PUBLIC_URL` | variable, optional | `https://hotdog-simulator.chilos.dev` by default |
   | `DEPLOY_KEEP` | variable, optional | releases kept, 5 by default |
   | `DEPLOY_SCOPE` | variable, optional | `user` (default) or `system` |
8. **Protect main**: Settings > Rules > Rulesets (or Branches), for `main`: require a pull request before merging and require the status check **`ci-ok`** (it can be picked once CI has run once). Then the first deploy: merge a pull request, or Actions > deploy > Run workflow.

### A merge, and going back

A pull request runs ci.yml; once `ci-ok` is green it can be merged. The merge is a push to main, which starts deploy.yml: the tests again on the merged commit, with the two packages built meanwhile (the browser builds' optimised link is the longest part), then the deploy (after an approval, if the environment asks for one), whose log and summary say which release went live and which was before it. A failed check at the public address rolls the server back by itself, and the run fails with the reason.

To go back by hand to the release before (the server keeps five):

```sh
tools/deploy/rollback.sh --identity hotdog-deploy hotdog@hotdog-simulator.chilos.dev '~/hotdog-simulator'            # the one before
tools/deploy/rollback.sh --status --identity hotdog-deploy hotdog@hotdog-simulator.chilos.dev '~/hotdog-simulator'   # the releases there
tools/deploy/rollback.sh --identity hotdog-deploy hotdog@hotdog-simulator.chilos.dev '~/hotdog-simulator' 20261119T183000Z-0.1.0-bf5e6ec
```

or, without SSH, Actions > deploy > Run workflow with the older commit (it is tested and built again). Then revert the bad change on main, or the next merge brings it back. A deploy by hand is the same script: `tools/build-all.sh linux web`, then `tools/deploy/deploy.sh --identity hotdog-deploy hotdog@hotdog-simulator.chilos.dev '~/hotdog-simulator' dist/` (its options are at its top). [tools/deploy/test-local.sh](tools/deploy/test-local.sh) runs the whole sequence on one machine, without a server: a folder as the server, the game's server as a background process, and with `CADDY=/path/to/caddy` the Caddy example in front: deploys, a release that fails the public checks and is rolled back, one whose server never answers, one refused before it goes live, pruning, and a rollback.

## The breakfast intro

Single player natively starts with the breakfast scene (`--skip-intro` goes straight into a round); in a browser it plays with `?intro` in the address. The fridge door opens on Maya, the breakfast chef, and her husband; they greet each other (a handshake, a hug, a laugh) while the hotdog's eye, from the tray, closes in on them and then on itself. Space (or the page's button) calls Maya: she reaches into the fridge, says her line, carries the tray to the counter and sets it on the cutting board; then she takes the knife, cuts the seal, the film peels back and the round begins. R restarts it. The scene's clock (`src/maya/choreography.rs`) is the one it always had: the beats begin at the same times, the hotdog's camera takes the same shots, and the knife and the film move as before.

Maya and her husband are the game's own, made entirely in code at load (`src/human/`): soft cartoon people in the hotdogs' style, with big eyes, a mouth that takes six shapes as they talk (`src/maya/act.rs` sets the shapes from their lines' letters), round limbs placed every frame by two-bone reaching so that a hand that holds the tray, the knife or the other's hand is exactly there, and planted steps. No model file, photograph or texture is loaded for them; their three cloth patterns are painted in code. At the Low and Lowest graphics levels they are remade with about a third of the triangles. The `counter-map` cargo feature, the early Counter Hold scene, uses her husband as its giant host behind the counter. See [the characters' notes](assets/CHARACTER_CREDITS.md).
